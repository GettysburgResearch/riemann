## 1. Exact operator

For `s>0`, put `p_s(x)=x^{-s}` and retain the exact shifted central operator

\[
(\mathscr C f)(x)=\sum_{k\ge1}[f(2kx-1)-f((2k+1)x)].
\]

PR #286 proves

\[
\mathscr Cp_s
=[1-\eta(s)]p_s
+\sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac{(s)_\ell}{\ell!}
2^{-s-\ell}\zeta(s+\ell)p_{s+\ell}.
\tag{L-32401.1}
\]

For the critical exponent lattice

\[
s=\frac12+h,\qquad h\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0},
\]

define the stronger coefficient norm

\[
\boxed{
\left\|\sum_{h\ge0}a_hp_{1/2+h}\right\|_{1/2}
=\sum_{h\ge0}|a_h|2^{-h}.
}
\tag{L-32401.2}
\]

This uses radius `r=1/2`, twice the radius `1/4` used in `L-28401`.

## 2. Elementary zeta bound

We need only

\[
\boxed{\zeta(3/2)<\frac83.}
\tag{L-32401.3}
\]

Indeed,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\zeta(3/2)
&=1+2^{-3/2}+3^{-3/2}+\frac18+5^{-3/2}
 +\sum_{n\ge6}n^{-3/2}\\
&<1+\frac{71}{200}+\frac{193}{1000}
 +\frac18+\frac9{100}+\frac{112}{125}\\
&=\frac{2659}{1000}<\frac83.
\end{aligned}
\tag{L-32401.4}
\]

The rational inequalities follow by squaring positive quantities; the tail uses

\[
\sum_{n\ge6}n^{-3/2}\le\int_5^\infty x^{-3/2}dx=\frac2{\sqrt5}<\frac{112}{125}.
\]

## 3. Weighted row sum at radius one half

For one source exponent `s`, the weighted absolute coefficient row sum in (L-32401.2) is

\[
 m(s)
=1-\eta(s)
+\sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac{(s)_\ell}{\ell!}
2^{-s-\ell}\zeta(s+\ell)2^{-\ell}.
\tag{L-32401.5}
\]

The alternating-series estimate gives

\[
1-\eta(s)\le2^{-s}.
\]

For `s>=1/2` and `ell>=1`, (L-32401.3) gives `zeta(s+ell)<8/3`. Therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
 m(s)
&<2^{-s}+\frac83 2^{-s}
 \left[(1-1/4)^{-s}-1\right]\\
&=\boxed{
B(s):=\frac83\left(\frac23\right)^s
      -\frac53\left(\frac12\right)^s.}
\end{aligned}
\tag{L-32401.6}
\]

On the exponent lattice,

\[
B(s+1)-B(s)
=-\frac89\left(\frac23\right)^s
 +\frac56\left(\frac12\right)^s<0,
\tag{L-32401.7}
\]

because the ratio of the positive term to the negative magnitude is

\[
\frac{15}{16}\left(\frac34\right)^s<1.
\]

Thus the maximum occurs at `s=1/2`.

## 5. Why the stronger radius matters

The radius `1/2` weights one additional inverse power by the same dyadic factor naturally generated by support halving. It is therefore closer to the square-root capacity / first-difference norms used by the discrete Cycle-Debt and coupled-boundary programmes than the earlier radius `1/4` norm.
