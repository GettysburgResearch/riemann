# Finiteness without linear independence when the real segment is clear

Let \(J_K\) be the integrated prime-ideal harmonic error in the [field sign criterion](zhao-field-sign-equivalence-nov18.md). Among *all* number fields having no real zero in \([1/2,1)\), only finitely many can have \(J_K(X)>0\) eventually. This assertion assumes neither a family GRH nor linear independence. It permits unbounded degrees and root discriminants of bounded or unbounded size. It does not identify every exception. In particular any sequence of distinct fields exhibiting eventual positivity outside a finite set must have real zeros in the stated segment.

More precisely, along any sequence of distinct fields with a clear real segment, the limiting inferior in \(X\) of \((\log X)J_K(X)/\sqrt X\) is at most \(-2+o(1)\) as \(|\operatorname{disc}K|\to\infty\). On failure of the individual RH it is negative infinity by the [Landau argument](zhao-field-sign-equivalence-nov18.md). For the individual-RH case we give the family argument, a specialization of the explicit-formula method of Tsfasman and Vlăduţ for asymptotically exact families.

## A small positive ordinate for arbitrarily growing degrees

Fix a sequence of distinct fields satisfying their own GRH, and write \(L=\log|\operatorname{disc}K|\). By Hermite–Minkowski, \(L\to\infty\), and \([K:\mathbb Q]/L\) is bounded by Minkowski and Stirling. On any subsequence take a further diagonal subsequence on which \((r_1+r_2)/L,r_2/L\) and \(\#\{\mathfrak p:N\mathfrak p=q\}/L\) for every positive rational prime power \(q\) converge. Denote the limits by \(u_0,u_1,\phi_q\). Each numerator in the last ratio is at most the degree. Count every zero with multiplicity, using signed ordinates.

For an even smooth compactly supported function \(F\), let \(h(t)=\int F(y)e^{ity}dy\). The Weil explicit formula, divided by \(L\), has a limit. The conductor gives \(F(0)\), the polar terms disappear for a fixed test, and the prime side has finitely many terms. The gamma side consists of the two usual real gamma factors with coefficients tending to \(u_a\). We first check absolute convergence before writing the limit as a density. Apply the same formula to \(F_R(y)=(1-|y|/R)_+\), whose transform is nonnegative and falls off quadratically, for each fixed \(R\ge1\). Piecewise linear tests follow by smoothing or the classical integrated explicit formula. The zero side is nonnegative on individual GRH. The gamma contribution is bounded above uniformly in \(R\): the Fourier transform has mass \(2\pi\) and is at most \(4/(Rt^2)\) away from zero, whereas the real digamma integrands are \(O(\log(2+|t|))\). The polar terms already vanish in the field limit taken first. Since \(F_R\uparrow1\),
\[
 \sum_q\frac{\phi_q\log q}{\sqrt q-1}<\infty.
\]
The resulting normalized zero distribution has continuous density
\[
 \frac1{2\pi}\left(1+\sum_{a=0}^1 u_a\{\Re\psi((1/2+a+it)/2)-\log\pi\}
 -2\sum_q\phi_q\log q\sum_{k\ge1}q^{-k/2}\cos(kt\log q)\right). \tag{1}
\]
Indeed the prime series is uniformly absolutely convergent and its pairing with \(h\) gives exactly the prime side above. To extend from the transform tests to compactly supported smooth functions of the ordinate, cut off their Schwartz inverse transforms smoothly; convergence holds in all Schwartz seminorms. The normalized zero measures have a uniform \(O(\log(2+|t|))\) bound on the mass in unit intervals. This follows by taking the real part of the pole-cancelled completed logarithmic derivative at \(2+it\): each zero in the interval costs a positive absolute constant, and the conductor, gamma and absolutely convergent Euler terms cost \(O(L+[K:\mathbb Q]\log(2+|t|))\). Even tests suffice because the measure is symmetric. Thus (1) is a nonnegative continuous density, in particular nonnegative at zero.

Its value at each \(t\ne0\) is strictly positive. Subtract its value at zero. Every prime term in the difference is nonnegative. For \(x>0\), \(\Re\psi(x+iy)-\psi(x)=\sum_{j\ge0}y^2/[(j+x)((j+x)^2+y^2)]\) is positive for \(y\ne0\). This proves the strict inequality when some \(u_a>0\). If both are zero, the degree divided by \(L\) tends to zero, forcing \(\phi_q=0\) for every \(q\); in this case the density is constant \(1/(2\pi)\). Testing any positive-ordinate open interval with a nonzero nonnegative smooth function shows it eventually contains a zero along the extracted subsequence. Contradiction to a missing interval on an original subsequence proves that the *least strictly positive ordinate* tends to zero along every such sequence. Central vanishing would not affect this last existence statement.

## The sign consequence

Assume now the central value is nonzero. On the individual GRH the limiting profile of \((\log X)J_K(X)/\sqrt X\) is \(P(t)=2+\sum_\rho e^{i\gamma t}/(1/4+\gamma^2)\) with \(t=\log X\). For any positive ordinate \(\gamma\) with multiplicity \(m_\gamma\), its Bohr average against \(1-\cos(\gamma t)\) is exactly \(2-m_\gamma/(1/4+\gamma^2)\). The multiplier is nonnegative of mean one. Therefore the limiting inferior of the profile is at most this number, without any independence of distinct ordinates. Use the least positive ordinate and \(m_\gamma\ge1\) to obtain the claimed asymptotic upper bound \(-2+o(1)\). The profile has mean 2, so positive values also recur. The finite case of negative discriminant \(-163\) admits a direct interval verification by the same Fourier coefficient argument.

## Allowing central vanishing in the triangle bound

Under the individual RH let \(m\) be the central multiplicity. The effective triangle-bound threshold for the integrated harmonic mean is \(B_K-8m<2\), since the central term always has positive phase. This enlarged sufficient condition can include fields outside the 2,580 entries. Nevertheless it holds for at most finitely many fields, even with arbitrarily growing degree and no conjecture on central ranks. More precisely \(m=o(B_K)\) along every sequence of distinct fields satisfying their individual RH, and \(B_K\asymp \log|\Delta|\) there. Thus if the distinct positive ordinates are also rationally independent, the normalized limiting inferior tends to negative infinity along any such sequence, since it equals \(2+8m-B_K\).

Here is an effective finiteness argument. The three-derivative bound at \(s=7/2\) in the [degree proof](zhao-number-fields-nov18.md) gives \(B_K\ge n/4-2\), with \(n=[K:\mathbb Q]\): the sum over gamma shifts exceeds \(1/2\) already from finitely many terms, and the two pole costs total less than 2. At \(s=2\), using \(\zeta_K'/\zeta_K\ge n\zeta'/\zeta\), the completed logarithmic derivative satisfies \(D_K(2)\ge L/2-1.5n\). The archimedean lower bound per real factor is \((\psi(1)-\log\pi)/2>-0.87\), and \(\zeta'(2)/\zeta(2)>-0.57\). On GRH, \(D_K(2)\le(3/2)B_K\) and \(B_K\le6D_K(2)\) by termwise comparison on the real ordinate axis. Dropping the nonpositive Euler term for the latter upper estimate gives \(B_K=O(L+n+1)\). In particular \(L\le 3B_K+3n\le15B_K+24\).

The preceding triangular Fourier test before taking its field limit gives for each \(R\ge1\)
\[
             mR\le L+C n+2h_R(i/2).
\]
The prime term is nonpositive and the gamma integrals are bounded above by an absolute computable constant \(C\) per factor, uniformly in \(R\), by the same unit-mass and quadratic-tail calculation. Since \(B_K\to\infty\), divide by \(B_K\), take the upper limit over fields, then send \(R\) to infinity. This gives \(m=o(B_K)\). To obtain an effective discriminant bound in the enlarged test, take any fixed \(R>16(15+4C)\) and keep the polar term. Then \(8m\le B_K/2+(8/R)(24+8C+2h_R(i/2))\). Hence \(B_K-8m\le2\) bounds \(B_K\), and the preceding inequalities bound both the discriminant and the degree. This does not assume simplicity of the noncentral zeros.
