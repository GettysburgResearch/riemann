# Which trace inequalities force the Frobenius weight circle?

Weil's 1941 proof needs one compatible sequence of Frobenius power traces. It extracts weight one from the positivity of the transpose pairing on correspondences. A finite-spectrum reformulation reduces that positivity to two-dimensional tests, or even to a one-sided upper bound on a density-one set of powers. A reciprocal integral polynomial with off-circle roots passes every prime-degree test. The classical zeta problem still needs a global spectral realization and a trace bound in that realization.

The earlier geometry audit separates rationality, duality and Deligne's pole-control argument, and supplies counterexamples to deriving purity from local self-duality or point counts. This note concerns the finite trace algebra of the single global Frobenius. It gives a smaller positivity test on that algebra and compares it with the geometric inputs in the three primary sources. ## 1. The finite-spectrum criterion

Let \(A\) be a multiset of \(d>0\) nonzero complex numbers counted with positive integer multiplicity. Suppose \(A\) is stable under both conjugation and the involution \(\alpha\mapsto \rho^2/\alpha\), with fixed \(\rho>0\). Normalize to \(z=\alpha/\rho\) and put \(s_n=\sum_z z^n\). Then \(s_0=d\) and \(s_n=s_{-n}\in\mathbb R\). On real Laurent polynomials in \(x\), define
\[
\tau(p)=\sum_z p(z),\qquad p^*(x)=p(x^{-1}).
\]
The quotient by polynomials vanishing on the distinct \(z\) is well defined under both operations.

The following are equivalent.

1. Every \(z\) lies on the unit circle.
2. \(\tau(pp^*)\ge0\) for all real polynomials \(p\).
3. The same inequality holds for \(p=a+b x^n\), for all \(a,b\in\mathbb R\) and \(n\ge1\).
4. There is a set \(S\) of positive integers of natural density one such that for every \(\epsilon>0\), \(s_n\le e^{\epsilon n}\) holds eventually as \(n\) tends to infinity through \(S\).

**Proof.** Under (1), \(z^{-1}=\bar z\), and the summands in (2) are \(|p(z)|^2\). Condition (2) gives (3). The quadratic in (3) has diagonal entries \(d\) and off-diagonal entry \(s_n\), so (3) is equivalent to \(|s_n|\le d\) for all \(n\). This gives (4).

To deduce (1) from (4), suppose \(R=\max|z|>1\). Write \(z/R=e^{i\theta_j}\) for the \(m\) top-modulus roots, with multiplicity. In the compact closure of the cyclic group generated by \(g=(e^{i\theta_j})_{j=1}^m\), take an open neighborhood \(U\) of the identity where each coordinate has real part greater than \(1/2\). Returns \(g^n\in U\) have bounded gaps. The translates \(g^{-j}U\), \(j\in\mathbb Z\), cover the compact group, so a finite subcover supplies a bounded correction \(j\) for each \(n\). The positive return times consequently have positive lower density, and their intersection with \(S\) is unbounded. Along these \(n\), \(s_n/R^n\ge m/2-o(1)\), since all submaximal terms are exponentially smaller. Taking \(\epsilon<\log R\) contradicts (4). Thus \(R\le1\), and reciprocal pairing forces all moduli to equal one. \(\square\)

The proof also gives \(\limsup_{n\to\infty}(\max(s_n,0))^{1/n}=R\), even restricted to density one. The recurrence argument needs the same finite multiset for all tested \(n\) and positive multiplicities at the maximal modulus. One can use any subset meeting the relevant return times at arbitrarily large \(n\). An upper bound along an arbitrary infinite set gives no such guarantee. Reciprocal pairing is only needed to turn the upper bound into equality. A nonzero root strictly inside the disk passes all upper-bound tests on its own.

### A finite alternative requiring more than two-term tests

Let \(r\) be the number of distinct normalized roots. Under the same pairing assumptions it suffices to test (2) on \(\deg p\le r-1\). If some \(z\) is off the circle, its conjugacy class \(\{z,\bar z\}\) is disjoint from \(\{z^{-1},\overline{z^{-1}}\}\). Prescribe values \(+1\) and \(-1\), respectively, on these classes, and zero on all other roots. Interpolation gives a unique polynomial of degree at most \(r-1\) with real coefficients, since the data respect conjugation. Then \(\tau(pp^*)\) is minus the total multiplicity in the two classes. Equivalently, the Toeplitz matrix \((s_{i-j})_{0\le i,j<r}\) certifies the weight circle if it is positive semidefinite. Its test imposes joint inequalities across the first \(r\) powers.

This criterion recovers the spectral multiset only. Traces of powers are insensitive to Jordan blocks. An operator claim about semisimplicity or unitarity on the original cohomology needs additional input.

## 2. A sparse test that misses off-circle roots

Set \(q=4\), use the weight-one radius \(\rho=2\), and take
\[
P(X)=(X^4+32)(X^4+8)=X^8+40X^4+256.
\]
Its roots have moduli \(2\cdot2^{1/4}\) and \(2\cdot2^{-1/4}\), both with multiplicity four. Reciprocal pairing \(\alpha\mapsto 4/\alpha\) exchanges the two factors, and complex conjugation preserves each. Every factor gives four roots at equal angular intervals, so \(s_n=0\) for \(4\nmid n\). In particular every prime extension degree passes the two-term positivity test. At \(n=4\), \(s_4=(-128-32)/16=-10\), violating \(|s_4|\le8\). At \(n=8\), \(s_8=17>8\). All unnormalized traces come from one integral companion matrix. This gives an algebraic counterexample to sparse sampling under duality and integrality.

There is also no prescribed finite list of two-term tests that works for all real reciprocal polynomials of fixed degree four. Choose \(\theta/\pi\) irrational, and start from two copies of \(e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta}\). Replace the roots by \(R^{\pm1}e^{\pm i\theta}\) with both signs independent. Then \(s_n=2(R^n+R^{-n})\cos(n\theta)\). At \(R=1\), \(|s_n|<4\) for each positive tested \(n\). Continuity preserves all the strict bounds for \(R>1\) sufficiently close to one. This finite-horizon example uses real coefficients.

## 3. The assumptions used in the three sources

**Weil 1941, pp. 345–347.** The relevant algebra is that of curve correspondences modulo degenerate terms. His linear trace \(\sigma\) satisfies \(\sigma(1)=2g\). The correspondence transpose \(x\mapsto x'\) obeys \(\sigma(xx')\ge0\), using the generic complementary correspondence and its degree. At Frobenius \(F\), the identity \(FF'=q\) turns this into \(*\) on the normalized power algebra. The trace identity \(\sigma(F^n)=1+q^n-\#X(\mathbb F_{q^n})\) identifies the same \(\sigma(F^n)\) with the reciprocal-root power sum at each \(n\). The functional equation supplies reciprocal pairing. Weil's interpolation on p. 347 proves the spectral conclusion and his note defers foundational details of correspondence theory in positive characteristic. Restricted to \(1,F^n\), the very same geometric form gives the exact bound \(|\sigma(F^n)|\le 2gq^{n/2}\). Thus the two-term criterion requires only the restriction of Weil's positivity, once the trace identity and reciprocity are available. [Primary text, pp. 345–347](../sources/classical63_automorphic_geometry/weil1941.pdf)

**Grothendieck, Standard Conjectures, §§2–4.** Section 2 formulates \(C(X)\), algebraicity of the cohomological Künneth projectors, with an \(\ell\)-independent cycle refinement used for independence. Section 3 formulates the Lefschetz assertion \(A(X)\), including surjectivity on algebraic classes, and \(B(X)\), algebraicity of the Lefschetz lowering operator. In particular \(B(X)\) implies the algebraicity part of \(C(X)\), using polynomials in Lefschetz operators. Section 4 formulates \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X)\), positivity on primitive algebraic classes. Remark (2) explicitly invokes **\(B(X)\) and \(\mathrm{Hdg}(X\times X)\)** for the Weil–Serre implication about an endomorphism scaling the polarization. The positivity is on the product because correspondences are classes on \(X\times X\). In an abstract trace proof the roles are separate. Degree isolation and a polarity relating adjoint and inverse on Frobenius powers supply the compatible trace operation. The sign comes from a positive intersection form on the relevant primitive algebraic pieces. Cohomological hard Lefschetz alone, algebraic diagonal projectors alone, or positive eigenvalues of a chosen duality matrix do not give the positive trace form. For the radius conclusion only, it would suffice to realize and control the required Frobenius power tests on the relevant degree, irrespective of positivity on unrelated cycles. This is a sufficiency reduction, not a derivation of those tests from a weaker general standard conjecture. [Primary text, §§2–4](../sources/classical63_automorphic_geometry/grothendieck1968.pdf)

**Katz–Messing 1974, § II Theorem 1 and § III Theorem 2.** The comparison takes Deligne's étale polynomials and their exact weights as input (D1), together with reconstruction from smooth sections of a Lefschetz pencil (D2). On the other theory it assumes Poincaré duality, high-degree weak Lefschetz, and an alternating zeta determinant formula for smooth projective absolutely irreducible varieties over finite extensions of \(\mathbb F_q\). Induction and D2 first yield degree-by-degree divisibilities away from the middle. Comparing alternating products then leaves a residual identity among polynomials supported at the three distinct radii for adjacent degrees. Disjointness forces trivial residual factors. This avoids a new Hodge-type positivity proof for the second theory. It imports weight information with geometric hypotheses that isolate degrees. Section III Theorem 2 constructs algebraic Künneth projectors as rational polynomials in the Frobenius graph because those degree polynomials have disjoint root sets. Here purity supplies projectors, which is the reverse direction from using degree projectors as an input to a positivity proof. [Primary text, pp. 73–77](../sources/classical63_automorphic_geometry/katzmessing1974.pdf)

A simpler abstract comparison is available if two theories already have matching **degree-by-degree** power traces on a density-one set. To prove this, group identical roots in the difference and omit zero coefficients. With signed coefficients the all-phases-near-one argument can cancel. Instead the mean square of the top-modulus trigonometric sum tends to the sum of the squared moduli of the nonzero grouped coefficients, by finite geometric sums. The sum is uniformly bounded. Removing a density-zero set leaves the same positive mean square. Thus the difference cannot be identically zero on a density-one set unless the multisets coincide. For one exact finite bound on the total number of distinct roots in the two lists, consecutive matching traces also suffice by the Vandermonde system. The alternating total trace is weaker because terms of adjacent degrees can cancel identically. Katz–Messing supplies a geometric way to prevent that cancellation.

## 4. What would have to be supplied for classical ζ?

The finite-field input is a single operator with powers indexed by extension degree, trace identity for all powers, and an adjoint tied to the inverse by a fixed weight. At a number-field prime, local Frobenius describes an individual Euler factor. Different residue cardinalities do not form successive powers of one such operator. Also the completed classical zeta function has infinitely many nontrivial zeros. It cannot be represented by a finite characteristic polynomial in \(q^{-s}\), whose zero set would be periodic in the imaginary direction. On an infinite spectrum, the finite recurrence argument cannot be applied without a trace and a theorem passing from the regularized tests to individual zeros. Local positivity of Euler factors cannot substitute for that theorem.

The next concrete question is whether a specific proposed global trace model for the completed ζ function determines one bilinear form on all prime-power test functions, and proves a positive two-by-two restriction sharing a single normalization. In a model with regularization, first require an exact expression for the two-by-two entries including the archimedean term. Then determine whether that estimate detects an isolated off-line zero without a sign assumption on the rest of the trace. The algebraic prime-degree example shows why testing selected scales alone cannot establish the requisite separation.
