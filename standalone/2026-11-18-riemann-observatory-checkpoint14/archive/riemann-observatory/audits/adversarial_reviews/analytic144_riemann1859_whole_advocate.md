# Riemann 1859 | Independent whole-memoir advocate reconstruction

**PAPER-S001.** The continuation, functional equation, completed function, prime-power transform and finite inversion reconstruct from the memoir. The count forecast, entire product and identification of the ordered zero series with contour inversion need steps beyond those printed. A modern reconstruction supplies them without RH and recovers the constant corrected by Genocchi in 1860. The conjecture about real zeros and the closing numerical remarks have their own scope. This is an independent advocate report for adjudication, with no change to the work’s canonical status.

## Pages 671–673 | Euler product, Hankel contour and the theta route

Riemann thanks the Academy and names Gauss and Dirichlet as motivation. In his starting formula \(n\) runs over the positive integers. For \(\Re s>1\), the product and series agree by absolute convergence and unique factorization. The Euler branch of \(\log\zeta\) is \(\sum_{p,m\ge1}p^{-ms}/m\). This also certifies nonvanishing in that open half-plane. With \(\Pi(s-1)=\Gamma(s)\), summing the gamma integrals gives \(\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)=\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}(e^x-1)^{-1}dx\) there.

For the continued expression use the contour \(H\) with inward upper lip and outward lower lip about the positive ray, joined by a fixed circle at zero smaller than \(2\pi\). Choose \(\log(-z)\) real on the negative real axis. The lips have arguments \(-\pi,+\pi\) for \(-z\), yielding the printed difference \(e^{-\pi i s}-e^{\pi i s}\) when \(\Re s>1\) and the circle is shrunk. Thus
\[
 2\sin(\pi s)\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)=i\int_H\frac{(-z)^{s-1}}{e^z-1}\,dz .
\]
Holding the small radius fixed makes the contour integral entire in \(s\) by exponential decay on the rays. Reflection replaces the product in front of \(\zeta\) by \(2\pi/\Gamma(1-s)\). This supplies meromorphic continuation, with the pole at 1 visible from the original Mellin expansion \((e^x-1)^{-1}=x^{-1}+O(1)\) at zero. The continued expression is the contour expression rather than the original divergent real integral. Ordinary continuation from the Euler half-plane fixes uniqueness across apparent integer singularities.

For \(\Re s<0\) close the exterior through rectangles of comparable width and height whose horizontal edges have imaginary parts \(\pm(2N+1)\pi\). The horizontal denominator is then bounded away from zero, as on the sufficiently far outer verticals. The remaining large boundary is \(O_s(R^{\Re s})\) and vanishes. Each exterior pole \(2\pi i n\), \(n\ne0\), contributes the negatively oriented residue \(-2\pi i(-2\pi i n)^{s-1}\). Pairing \(n,-n\) gives the printed \((2\pi)^s\zeta(1-s)((-i)^{s-1}+i^{s-1})\) on the right. The last factor is \(2\sin(\pi s/2)\). Reflection and duplication of gamma yield the standard completed functional equation. The negative even zeros follow because the sine vanishes and the other factors in this half-plane are finite and nonzero there. The same negative-even assertion can be verified directly on p. 672: at \(s=-2m\), \(m\ge1\), the two lips cancel and the residue around zero would require a positive even power \(z^{2m}\) in \((e^z-1)^{-1}\). There is none because \((e^z-1)^{-1}+1/2\) is odd. This is the route given in the 1892 note on p. 154.

For the second route let \(\psi_\theta(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-\pi n^2x}\). The elementary gamma transform first gives \(\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)=\int_0^\infty \psi_\theta(x)x^{s/2-1}dx\) on \(\Re s>1\). Jacobi’s identity \(1+2\psi_\theta(x)=x^{-1/2}(1+2\psi_\theta(1/x))\) evaluates the lower part. Its constant part is \(1/(s-1)-1/s\). Substituting \(x\mapsto1/x\) only in the lower theta term gives
\[
 \pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)=\frac1{s(s-1)}
 +\int_1^\infty \psi_\theta(x)[x^{s/2-1}+x^{-(s+1)/2}]dx .
\]
The integral on the right is entire by local uniform exponential convergence, and symmetric under \(s\mapsto1-s\). The original p. 673 and 1892 p. 147 have the correct intermediate term \(\int_0^1\psi_\theta(1/x)x^{(s-3)/2}dx\). Both standalone Wilkins versions instead give \(\int_1^\infty\) with that same integrand. At \(s=2\) the latter integrand tends to \(1/2\) by Jacobi, hence diverges. The final split identity is correct in both versions. This remains a manifestation discrepancy, separate from the mathematics of the German print.

## Page 674 | The completed function, growth, zeros and conjecture

Riemann’s \(\xi(t)\) is the modern \(\Xi(t)=\xi_{\mathrm{mod}}(1/2+it)\), with \(\xi_{\mathrm{mod}}(s)=\tfrac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)\). The first printed cosine formula follows by combining the two theta powers. In detail take \(v=\tfrac12\log x\) and \(g(v)=e^{v/2}\psi_\theta(e^{2v})\). It gives \(\Xi(t)=\tfrac12-2(t^2+1/4)\int_0^\infty g(v)\cos(tv)dv\). Differentiating Jacobi at 1 gives \(g'(0)=-1/4\). Integrating twice then gives \(\Xi(t)=2\int_0^\infty(g''-g/4)\cos(tv)dv\), which becomes the second printed kernel \(4(x^{3/2}\psi_\theta'(x))'x^{-1/4}\) against \(\cos(t\log x/2)\,dx\). Each differentiated Gaussian in this last kernel is positive on \(x\ge1\), its sign coming from \(\pi n^2x-3/2>0\). Thus \(\Xi(0)>0\) and, using \(\cosh\), there are no zeros on the imaginary \(t\)-axis. Since the first integral at \(t=0\) is positive, \(\Xi(0)<1/2\). Meanwhile \(\Xi(i/2)=\xi_{\mathrm{mod}}(0)=1/2\).

The cosine representation is entire, even and real on the real line. Expand the cosine on compact parameter sets to justify the power series in \(t^2\). The rapid decay also bounds the maximum modulus by \(\exp(O(R\log(R+2)))\) for \(|t|\le R\), since the log-coordinate kernel decays like a polynomial exponential times \(e^{-c e^{2v}}\). Splitting into the tail and a bounded log-coordinate interval, or substituting \(y=e^{2v}\) and bounding the resulting gamma integral, proves order at most one. This quantitative growth statement is a modern supplement to the printed entire-function observation.

The Euler product and the functional equation give only \(0\le\Re s\le1\) for the completed zeros by the arguments printed, or \(|\Im t|\le1/2\). Nonvanishing on \(\Re s=1\) at nonzero ordinate is a later step, and is not needed for the conditionality classification here. The original wording's imaginary quantities should be understood as the two horizontal edges \(t=\text{real}\pm i/2\). The argument principle legitimately counts multiplicities in a rectangle whose boundary avoids zeros. Riemann states the smooth main terms \((T/(2\pi))\log(T/(2\pi))-T/(2\pi)\) and describes an error as a fraction of order \(1/T\) of the displayed integral. The modern \(O(\log T)\) remainder is consistent with a relative reading. The memoir provides no separate boundary argument establishing that remainder.

One modern completion enlarges to zero-free verticals \(\Re s=3,-2\) and uses the symmetries to write \(N(T)=1+\vartheta(T)/\pi+S(T)\) at nonordinate heights, with \(\vartheta(T)=\Im\log\Gamma(1/4+iT/2)-(T/2)\log\pi\). Here \(S=\arg\zeta(1/2+iT)/\pi\) is the continuous argument from the right along the usual broken path. Stirling gives the two stated terms and the modern \(7/8\) after the initial 1. To bound \(S\), on the horizontal segment use Jensen for \((\zeta(z+iT)+\zeta(z-iT))/2\) in two fixed nested disks centered at 3, with smaller disk containing \([1/2,3]\). Its center is bounded away from zero by absolute convergence, and its larger boundary has polynomial growth by the usual fixed-strip zeta bounds. Thus its real zeros along the segment number \(O(\log T)\). Between those crossings the argument has bounded net variation in a half-plane. The result extends to endpoint counts by one-sided limits, also giving the unit-height count \(O(\log T)\). This is a reconstruction with later uniform estimates, not a claim that the memoir supplied them. The number of *real* \(t\)-zeros was reported approximately and left without a strict proof. The statement that all are probably real is explicitly conjectural, and neither the paired product nor the explicit formula needs it. [Modern count dependency, Titchmarsh §§9.3–9.4](../../sources/equivalence_round2/titchmarsh1986.pdf)

## Pages 675–677 | Paired product and the prime-power inverse transform

Take one \(\alpha\) from each pair \(\{\alpha,-\alpha\}\), with \(\Re\alpha>0\), counting multiplicity. The count or Jensen growth bound gives \(\sum|\alpha|^{-2}<\infty\). Thus \(P(t)=\prod_\alpha(1-t^2/\alpha^2)\) converges normally. Riemann argues that the difference of logarithms is constant, using growth proportional to \(t\log t\) and evenness, but does not establish a globally controlled branch for that argument near zeros or a zero-free quotient growth theorem. The modern proof applies Hadamard factorization to the order-at-most-one \(\Xi\). Pairing the opposite genus-one elementary factors cancels the two exponential pieces. The remaining factor is \(e^{A+Bt}\), with \(B=0\) by evenness, and \(e^A=\Xi(0)\). This supplies the lemma needed for the memoir’s product. It is separate from the 1896 boundary-zero result and from the historical credit for proving the formula rigorously.

Riemann’s \(F(x)\) is \(\pi_0(x)=\#\{p<x\}+\tfrac12\mathbf 1_{x\ \mathrm{prime}}\). His \(f(x)\) is
\[
 J_0(x)=\sum_{m\ge1}\pi_0(x^{1/m})/m
       =\sum_{p^m<x}1/m+\tfrac12\sum_{p^m=x}1/m .
\]
The transform \(\log\zeta(s)/s=\int_1^\infty J_0(x)x^{-s-1}dx\), \(\Re s>1\), follows by integrating the absolutely convergent Euler logarithm term by term. For \(a>1\), the log-coordinate function \(e^{-au}J_0(e^u)\) is integrable and of bounded variation: the exponentially weighted jumps sum absolutely, and the ordinary decrease between jumps contributes a finite integral. Dirichlet–Jordan Fourier inversion therefore yields the printed symmetric Perron formula with exact midpoint value at prime powers. In the source’s general sine–cosine explanation the two separated inversions actually contain the even and odd parts of the log-coordinate profile, as 1892 note (2) on p. 154 records. Only their sum has the stated general inversion. The specialized inversion for the prime count is valid.

Integration by parts on the original \(\log\zeta(s)/s\) inverse is justified at finite heights followed by the limit: on \(\Re s=a\) the Euler logarithm is bounded, so the boundary term is \(O_{a,x}(1/T)\) for \(x>1\). Riemann explicitly recognizes that separating the undifferentiated constituent logarithms in infinite integrals is invalid. The Euler gamma product for \(\Gamma(1+s/2)\) reproduces p. 677. Its linear-in-\(s\) pieces give constants after division by \(s\) and vanish on differentiation. The original p. 676 substitution displays the zero logarithm with no visible sum sign, which the Wilkins versions supply. On original p. 677 the upward residue display with denominator \((\beta-s)\beta\) needs a minus sign in front of the integral to equal \(x^\beta/\beta\) for \(x>1\). The numerator’s residue at \(s=\beta\) has sign minus. Both Wilkins versions include the needed minus, and the following p. 678 expression already has the correct minus for the inverse of the undifferentiated logarithm. These are local notation/normalization observations, separate from the known constant correction.

## Pages 677–679 | Complex logarithmic integral, ordered limits and the constant

Put \(u=\log x>0\). For a complex \(\beta\) with \(\Im\beta\ne0\), on \(\Re s>\max(0,\Re\beta)\) choose \(\log(1-s/\beta)=\log(s-\beta)-\log(-\beta)\), with principal logarithms on the right and the value corresponding to continuation from \(s=0\). The inverse Laplace transform of \(-\log(s-\beta)/s\) at \(u>0\) is
\[
 \gamma_E+\log u+\int_0^u\frac{e^{\beta v}-1}{v}dv .
\]
Indeed the inverse for \(-\log s/s\) is \(\gamma_E+\log u\), by differentiating the gamma transform, and the difference follows from \(\log s-\log(s-\beta)=\int_0^\infty e^{-sv}(e^{\beta v}-1)dv/v\). Adding \(\log(-\beta)\) gives the inverse of \(-\log(1-s/\beta)/s\), denoted \(K_\beta(u)\). For nonreal \(\beta\) it is \(\mathrm{Ei}(\beta u)-i\pi\,\mathrm{sgn}(\Im\beta)\) with principal analytic \(\mathrm{Ei}\); the conjugate constants cancel in the symmetric zero sum. This is the decaying upper/lower continuation contemplated on p. 678. For a negative real \(\beta\) the same transform gives real \(\mathrm{Ei}(\beta u)\) with integral normalized at minus infinity. The pole term uses the branch \(-\log(s-1)/s\), real on \(s>1\), and gives \(\mathrm{Ei}(u)=\operatorname{PV}\int_0^x dt/\log t\), not the integral normalized at 2. In the \(x\)-plane detouring above or below 1 changes the lateral integral by \(2\pi i\), and its average is the real principal value. For a nonreal zero, the expression \(\operatorname{Li}(x^\rho)\) denotes the continuation in \(\rho\log x\), not a new principal logarithm of the complex number \(x^\rho\).

For \(\rho_\pm=1/2\pm i\alpha\), separation of a quadratic factor gives the exact identity
\[
 1+(s-1/2)^2/\alpha^2
 =(1+1/(4\alpha^2))(1-s/\rho_+)(1-s/\rho_-).
\]
The normalization factors are absolutely convergent. Evaluating the product at \(s=0\) shows \(\Xi(0)\prod_\alpha(1+1/(4\alpha^2))=\xi_{\mathrm{mod}}(0)=1/2\). Their real logarithmic sum combined with \(\log\Xi(0)\) is \(-\log2\). Conjugate grouping fixes the Euler branch on the positive real \(s>1\) and avoids spurious multiples of \(2\pi i\). Genocchi derives this same normalization explicitly on pp. 57–58 and gives \(-\log2\) in (11). The 1892 editorial note on p. 155 likewise states \(\log(1/2)\). The original p. 678 and the end of p. 680 use \(\log\Xi(0)\). The recovered difference is the known historical correction. The editors’ speculation about a typo involving \(\zeta(0)\) is not used as a proof or as established history. [Genocchi original, equation (11)](../../sources/riemann_constant_skeptic_round4/genocchi1860_pp52-59.pdf) · [1892 notes](../../sources/history_round4/riemann1892_collected.pdf)

Here is a modern way to make the infinite inverse and the zero order precise. On the fixed line \(\Re s=a>1\), the paired, \(s=0\)-normalized zero-product logarithms have summed absolute size \(O_a((1+|s|)\log^2(2+|s|))\). For \(|\rho|>2|s|\) this follows by Taylor expansion and \(1/\rho+1/(1-\rho)=1/[\rho(1-\rho)]\), with the tail of \(\sum|\rho|^{-2}\). The remaining \(O(|s|\log |s|)\) terms are individually \(O_a(\log(2+|s|))\), since \(|s-\rho|\ge a-1\) and arguments remain bounded on these branches. The gamma product has the same kind of polynomial majorant after its linear terms are retained. Pairing against a smooth compact function of \(u>0\) gains arbitrary inverse powers on the line, so these bounds justify termwise inversion in distributions. Linear terms of the logarithm contribute masses at \(u=0\) after division by \(s\), outside the test support. Negative even contributions yield \(-\sum_{n\ge1}\mathrm{Ei}(-2nu)=\int_x^\infty dy/[(y^2-1)y\log y]\) by ordinary absolute summation for fixed \(u>0\).

To pass from this distributional identity to the raw height order, use the classical von Mangoldt formula for the half-weighted Mangoldt prefix \(\psi_0(x)\), in its usual symmetric height convention, and its compact-test version. The two inputs may be obtained from Perron inversion of \(-\zeta'/\zeta(s)/s\) by contour displacement, with the poles at 1, the nontrivial zeros, 0 and the negative even integers, and a midpoint at each jump. For nonreal \(\rho\), a horizontal integral from \(-\infty+i\Im(\rho u)\) to \(\rho u\), or one integration by parts there, gives
\[
 K_\rho(u)=\frac{e^{\rho u}}{\rho u}+O_I((1+|\Im\rho|)^{-2})
\]
uniformly on any fixed positive compact \(u\)-interval \(I\), since \(0\le\Re\rho\le1\). The remainder is continuous and sums normally by the zero count. The main term is the known raw zero series for \(\psi_0(e^u)\) divided by \(u\) after subtracting its smooth terms. Thus the inverted expression is represented pointwise by a function with midpoint values at the prime powers and is continuous between them. The distributional identity determines that representative, giving for every fixed \(x>1\)
\[
 J_0(x)=\operatorname{Li}(x)-\lim_{T\to\infty}\sum_{|\Im\rho|\le T}\operatorname{Li}(x^\rho)
     +\int_x^\infty\frac{dy}{(y^2-1)y\log y}-\log 2 .
\]
The sum and the real branches have the conventions above. The spectral height and original ordering by \(|\alpha|\) give the same limit because \(|\alpha|-\Re\alpha=O(1/\Re\alpha)\) in the strip, and the discrepancy costs at most \(O_x(\log T/T)\) using the unit count and the asymptotic for \(K_\rho\).

The corresponding separated contour limit on pp. 678–679 can now be justified after normalization. Integrate the original \(\log\zeta/s\) by parts as above. For the gamma quotient the derivative has leading \(1/(2s)\) up to sign and an absolutely integrable \(O_a(\log(2+|t|)/(1+t^2))\) remainder by Stirling, so multiplying by \(e^{su}\) gives a convergent raw integral on \(u>0\). The pole logarithm and the constant have absolutely integrable derivatives. This isolates a convergent zero-piece contour integral. Distributional inversion of its \(s=0\)-normalized product, or the difference of the already identified total and the smooth pieces, gives the same ordered zero series. More explicitly, if \(C=\log(1/2)-\log\Xi(0)\), the centered quadratic logarithms equal the normalized zero logarithms plus \(C\). In the positive-sign zero-only derivative integral on p. 678, divided by \(u\), this contributes \(-C\), since \((C/s)^{\prime}=-C/s^2\) and \(\mathcal L^{-1}(s^{-2})(u)=u\). Thus the raw centered integral represents the ordered zero sum minus \(C\) on these branches. In the full formula that becomes the historically corrected constant. The 1892 note on p. 154 quotes a draft letter saying the proof was not fully executed and singling out both the asserted real-zero count and the agreement of the zero-series and contour limits as relying on an unpublished expansion. The contemporary proof-status observation is reported from the editors, without a new identification of an autograph. [Original editorial note, p. 154](../../sources/history_round4/riemann1892_p154-170.png) · [Later proof context](../../sources/history_round4/mangoldt1894_laugel1896.pdf) · [Classical explicit formula dependencies](../../sources/equivalence_round2/titchmarsh1986.pdf)

The memoir’s warning about rearrangement is material. For example the modern unconditional equidistribution of \(\gamma u/(2\pi)\bmod1\) at fixed \(u>0\) follows from the fixed-\(x\) Landau sum and \(\sum_{0<\gamma\le T}|\beta-1/2|=o(T\log T)\). The latter follows from Littlewood’s formula and the critical-line mean-square upper bound, and removes the factor \(e^{\beta m u}\) in each fixed Weyl frequency \(m\). The Landau main term is \(O(T)\), smaller than \(N(T)\). For positive-density subsets on which the sine is bounded away from zero, the real conjugate blocks have leading term \(2e^{\beta u}\sin(\gamma u)/(\gamma u)\), of either sign, with an absolutely summable error. There is divergent absolute mass in these real blocks by the zero count on dyadic ranges. One may group the full quartet if preserving \(\rho\leftrightarrow1-\rho\). Thus a later theorem and the real rearrangement theorem justify the warning and the possibility of changing the value by reordering. The memoir did not prove that additional equidistribution input. ## Pages 679–680 | Recovering primes, oscillation and the announced approximation

The printed restriction to squarefree \(m\) with sign \((-1)^{\#\{p:p\mid m\}}\) gives exactly \(\pi_0(x)=\sum_{m\ge1}\mu(m)J_0(x^{1/m})/m\). At fixed \(x\) the arithmetic sums are finite because the functions vanish below 2. Substitution yields coefficients \(n^{-1}\sum_{m\mid n}\mu(m)\) and keeps the midpoint weights at all powers. This is a rigorous arithmetic identity.

For a finite zero truncation differentiate in \(x\). Each pair contributes \(-2x^{-1/2}\cos(\alpha\log x)/\log x\) and the trivial-zero integral contributes \(-1/[x(x^2-1)\log x]\). The pole gives \(1/\log x\). This is a correct derivative of the truncated continuous expression, also for complex \(\alpha\) if the requisite reflected/conjugate terms are retained. It is an approximation to the local weighted prime-power density in the heuristic sense of the memoir. The exact derivative of the arithmetic step function is an atomic measure. There is no termwise classical derivative of the infinite conditionally convergent zero sum and no uniform approximation bound established by this discussion.

The displayed nonperiodic terms follow from the first squarefree exponents in the Möbius formula, with the signs at 2,3,5,6,7 as printed. Their leading negative correction \(-\tfrac12\operatorname{Li}(\sqrt x)\) explains a tendency of the smooth part, not a bound on the oscillatory sum. The statement about discrepancies on the scale \(x^{1/2}\) is an announced inference from this formal decomposition and numerical comparison. It is not a proved unconditional error term, and a global indefinite inequality \(\operatorname{Li}(x)>\pi(x)\) would be false by the later oscillation theorem. Riemann attributes to the Gauss–Goldschmidt calculation through three million a persistent sign on the range mentioned and suggests tracking the zero terms in future counts. The German “schon vom ersten Hunderttausend an” is “already from the first hundred thousand onward,” while Wilkins p. 9 says “in the first hundred thousand.” This is a translation wording distinction rather than a new check of those historical tables. The assertion that \(J_0\) looks smoother in the first hundred is a numerical impression. It uses the same printed \(\log\Xi(0)\), so the constant in a modern implementation remains Genocchi’s.
