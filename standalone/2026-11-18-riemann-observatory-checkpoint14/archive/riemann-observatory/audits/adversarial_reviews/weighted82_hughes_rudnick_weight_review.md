# Hughes–Rudnick’s continuous-height averaging weight

I read the four-page 2002 journal paper in full and the longer arXiv v1, including its definitions, explicit formula, smooth and oscillatory parts, moment theorem, and matching proof. Both literally allow a nonnegative mass-one weight with compact Fourier transform and omit a tail condition. The translated-weight witness gives an infinite first average under those literal assumptions. A rapidly decreasing weight is consistent with the authors’ intended localized average. More generally, adding \(\int w(u)\log^m(2+|u|)\,du<\infty\) suffices for the first \(m\) moments in the same strict Fourier window. The repair below explicitly handles odd orders without assuming an extra available moment of the prime sum. None of this changes the historical antecedent for the separated prime-product mechanism and finite Gaussian moment window. No canonical verified flag is proposed here. ## Literal definitions and the potential convention

C.P. Hughes and Z. Rudnick, *Linear statistics for zeros of Riemann’s zeta function*, C. R. Math. **335** (2002), 667–670, DOI 10.1016/S1631-073X(02)02541-4, defines its average on p. 668, just before Theorem 1.1. The longer arXiv:math/0208220v1, dated 28 August 2002, uses the same definition on p. 2, with Lemma 2.3 and Proposition 2.4 on p. 5 and Theorem 2.6 on p. 7. In both, \(w\ge0\), \(\int w=1\), and \(\widehat w\) is compactly supported. The words requiring a smooth compactly supported **Fourier transform** concern the statistic \(f\), not \(w\). The actual integrated variable is \(u=(\tau-T)/H\) on the whole real line, with \(H=T^a\), \(0<a\le1\). No bounded integration range, compact support in \(u\), Schwartz condition, or decay bound for \(w\) appears. The intended description of centers near \(T\) is motivation and does not imply the missing quantitative tail statement. Smoothness of \(w\) alone would not help. Even \(\tau/T\to1\) in probability when \(a<1\) allows the witness, since \(T^{a-1}u\to0\) in probability for any fixed integrable probability density. A convention that \(w\) is Schwartz or that \(\widehat w\) itself lies in \(C_c^\infty\) would exclude the witness, but neither version states that convention. The paper separately uses the explicit condition \(\widehat f\in C_c^\infty\).

The journal’s first unsupported step is the averaging in the proof of Lemma 2.2 on p. 669. In the longer version Lemma 2.3 directly assumes the needed logarithmic average when replacing its \(\tau\)-integral by \(\log T+O(1)\). Prime-sum localization and fixed-height convergence of the zero series do not imply this integrability.

## Literal counterexample at every fixed \(T>1\)

Choose a real even \(C_c^\infty\) Fourier bump with nonzero integral, take its inverse Fourier transform, square it on the real line, and normalize to get a nonnegative Schwartz \(w_0\) of mass one with compact Fourier support. Then
\[
 w(u)=\sum_{n\ge1}2^{-n}w_0(u-B_n),\qquad B_n=\exp(4^n)
\]
has mass one. It is smooth, since the series of each fixed derivative converges uniformly. The \(L^1\) convergence gives \(\widehat w(\xi)=\widehat w_0(\xi)\sum_n2^{-n}e^{-2\pi iB_n\xi}\), with the same support bound. The support can be made as small as desired by the original bump choice. Take a bounded interval on which \(w_0\) has fixed positive mass. Its translate by \(B_n\) gives a lower bound of order \(4^n\) for the logarithmic expectation of the \(n\)-th translate. Tonelli and \(\sum 2^{-n}4^n=\infty\) show \(\int w\log(2+|u|)=\infty\). A symmetric mixture of the positive and negative translates gives the same conclusion if evenness is desired. For fixed \(T,H>0\), replacing \(\log(2+|u|)\) by \(\log(1+|T+Hu|)\) preserves the divergence.

Choose a real even \(f\) with \(\widehat f\in C_c^\infty\), support strictly inside the claimed window and \(I=\int f>0\). It may be taken as the square of a real even bandlimited Schwartz function, with a Fourier bump supported in half the desired window. Its Fourier representation gives an entire extension rapidly decreasing in real direction on every fixed horizontal strip. At fixed \(T\) the zero arguments in the explicit formula lie in one such strip, since the zeta zeros are in \(0<\Re\rho<1\). The ordinary unit-window zero count and this decay give absolute convergence of \(N_f(\tau)\) for every real \(\tau\) and local uniform convergence in \(\tau\). The zero set's reflection across the critical line conjugates the scaled ordinates, so the statistic is real even without RH. The compactly supported function \(g\) in the explicit formula has the smooth multiplier \(e^{-i\tau u}\) for every fixed \(\tau\); there is no limitation of (2)–(4) to centers near \(T\).

The polar evaluations \(f(\frac{\log T}{2\pi}(\pm i/2-\tau))\) remain bounded as \(\tau\) varies at fixed \(T\) by the same horizontal-strip estimate. The oscillatory term is a finite sum over \(n\le T^\sigma\) for a fixed Fourier support radius \(\sigma\), hence bounded at fixed \(T\). It remains bounded under averaging by any mass-one weight. For the smooth integral, put \(L=\log T\) and substitute \(r=\tau+2\pi x/L\). The digamma estimate \(\Omega(r)=\log(1+|r|)+O(1)\) and the two triangle inequalities give
\[
 |\log(1+|\tau+v|)-\log(1+|\tau|)|\le\log(1+|v|).
\]
Consequently the smooth integral is \(I\log(1+|\tau|)/L+O_f(1/L)\) uniformly for \(T\ge2\), all real \(\tau\). This bound uses \(\int |f(x)|\log(2+|x|)\,dx<\infty\). Adding the other pieces gives \(N_f(\tau)=I\log(1+|\tau|)/L+O_{f,T}(1)\), uniformly in \(\tau\). Thus the first average equals \(+\infty\), for every fixed \(T\ge2\), and the other terms cannot cancel it. This is a literal counterexample for \(m=1\) and indeed shows that, at positive \(I\), the \(m\)-th absolute average at fixed \(T\) requires a finite \(m\)-th logarithmic moment. No RH or assumption on zeta zero locations beyond the standard critical strip was used.

## Sufficient moment hypothesis and the arithmetic input

Retain the original assumptions, add \(\int w(u)\log^m(2+|u|)\,du<\infty\), and fix \(\sigma<2a/m\) with \(\operatorname{supp}\widehat f\subset[-\sigma,\sigma]\). Fourier inversion gives a bounded continuous representative of \(w\), since \(\widehat w\) is bounded and compactly supported. Let \(S_f\) denote the oscillatory sum and \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) the average. Against any fixed nonnegative bandlimited integrable weight in \(u\), the moments \(\langle S_f^j\rangle\), \(j\le m\), have the standard Gaussian limit after normalization by the mass of that weight. The mean is eventually zero. Indeed the Fourier cutoff leaves only balanced products: for integers \(M>N\ge1\), \(\log(M/N)\ge 1/(2N)\), while \(N\le\sqrt{MN}\le T^{j\sigma/2}\). This exceeds the allowed denominator scale in \(|\log(M/N)|\ll H^{-1}\) when \(T\) is large. The factor \(\widehat w(0)\) is exactly its mass. No tail estimate on \(w\) is needed for this step.

For a fully explicit bound on the surviving terms, group equal underlying primes, writing each integer as \(p^{k_i}\). In each balanced prime block the signs split the exponents into two sums with equal value. A block of size two must have opposite signs with equal exponents. Every block of size \(j\ge3\) has total exponent at least four. Its absolute contribution is \(O_j(L^{-j}\sum_p(\log p)^j p^{-2})\), even after summing the possible exponents, by their geometric decay. The size-two blocks with exponent at least two are \(O(L^{-2})\) as well. The remaining paired prime blocks give Wick's combinatorics and the prime-number-theorem variance \(2\int_0^\infty v\widehat f(v)^2dv\). Duplicated underlying primes between the nominal pairs have negligible weight by another \(\sum p^{-2}\) estimate. When a second moment is among those required, \(\sigma<2a/m\le1\), so the variance agrees with the \(\min(|v|,1)\) formula. For \(m=1\), only the stated first moment is asserted. This argument also gives a uniform bound for the nonnegative \(S_f^{2j}\) averages whenever \(2j\le m\).

## Passing from the finite prime sum to the zero statistic

Write \(K=T/H\ge1\), \(v=u+K\), so \(\tau=Hv\). Let \(\Delta(u)\) be the smooth integral in (3) less \(I\). The preceding uniform gamma estimate implies
\[
 |\Delta(u)|\ll_f L^{-1}\bigl(1+\log(2+|u|)+\log_+(1/(|v|+H^{-1}))\bigr).
\]
For the positive logarithmic difference use \(H\le T\). For the negative difference, outside \(|v|<K/2\) its magnitude is at most \(\log 2\). Inside, its size is at most \(\log K+\log_+(1/(|v|+H^{-1}))\), and \(|u|\ge K/2\) controls \(\log K\).

To bound all mixed terms \(|S_f|^r|\Delta|^j\), \(j\ge1,\ r+j\le m\), one needs only even prime-sum moments through order \(m\). A direct Hölder argument using solely the \(L^m\) norm of \(\Delta\) would request an absolute \(m\)-th moment of \(S_f\) for odd \(m\), which the signed \(m\)-th calculation does not give. The following two estimates avoid that issue.

1. **Logarithmic tails in \(u\).** Choose a Schwartz \(\psi\) with \(\widehat\psi=1\) on \(\operatorname{supp}\widehat w\), hence \(w=w*\psi\). There exists a nonnegative bandlimited integrable \(\kappa\gg(1+|u|)^{-2}\). For example sum the squared normalized sinc at \(u\) and at \(u-1/2\); their squared numerators sum to one and their zeros do not coincide. The logarithmic triangle inequality and rapid decrease of \(\psi\) give
\[
 w(u)(1+\log^j(2+|u|))\ll
   [\kappa*(w(\cdot)(1+\log^j(2+|\cdot|)))](u).
\]
The convolution is a nonnegative bandlimited \(L^1\) weight of fixed finite mass. Apply its bounded even prime-sum moment at \(2\lceil r/2\rceil\le m\), then Hölder with that finite mass to obtain a bounded mixed integral. The case \(r=0\) is immediate.

2. **The moving singularity at \(v=0\).** Fix \(0<\varepsilon<a-m\sigma/2\). For every \(T^{-\varepsilon}\le\delta\le1\), majorize \({\bf1}_{|v|\le\delta}\) by a nonnegative bandlimited bump \(B(v/\delta)\) of mass \(O(\delta)\), with a fixed \(B\ge1\) on \([-1,1]\). The effective frequency window is \(H\delta\), still larger than \(T^{m\sigma/2}\) by a fixed power. The preceding exact separation argument is uniform in \(\delta\) and the center. Its even moment under the bump is \(O(\delta)\) and Hölder gives \(\int_{|v|\le\delta}|S_f|^rdu\ll\delta\). On the annuli with radii \(e^{-\ell}\ge T^{-\varepsilon}\) sum the bounds \(O((1+\ell)^je^{-\ell})\). In the core \(|v|<T^{-\varepsilon}\), the regularized logarithm is bounded by \(\log H\), costing \(O(T^{-\varepsilon}L^j)\). Boundedness of \(w\) suffices in this entire region.

Together these bounds give \(\langle |S_f|^r|\Delta|^j\rangle=O(L^{-j})\). They prove the mixed smooth comparison at every \(k\le m\) with an additive error, also when the target centered Gaussian moment vanishes.

Finally let \(P_f(\tau)\) be the sum of the two polar terms. Fourier integration by parts at heights \(\pm L/(4\pi)\) gives \(|P_f(\tau)|\ll_A T^{\sigma/2}(1+|\tau|)^{-A}\) for any fixed \(A\). For bounded \(\tau\) use the direct Fourier integral; on \(|\tau|\ge1\) the derivatives cost \(L^A\), absorbed by the real-direction integration gain \(L^{-A}\). The oscillatory sum has \(\|S_f\|_\infty\ll T^{\sigma/2}\) by \(\Lambda(n)\le\log n\) and the finite range. Thus each mixed integral containing at least one polar factor, with total degree at most \(m\), costs \(O(T^{m\sigma/2}/H)=o(1)\) by \(\|w\|_\infty\), the \(d\tau/H\) scaling, and the rapid decay. Extra powers of the smooth background grow at most as powers of \(1+\log(2+|\tau|)\), which are absorbed by that decay. This controls polar products rather than relying on the small signed mean of the individual evaluations. The signed first polar average can also be bounded directly by the paper’s Fourier formula and compact support of \(\widehat w\), which is legitimate by Fubini under the literal \(L^1\) assumptions.

Consequently the repaired \(k\)-th moment is the expectation of \((I+S_f)^k\) up to \(o(1)\) for every \(k\le m\), giving exactly the original Gaussian moment statement in its strict support window. Schwartz \(w\) with the original Fourier constraint is an easy sufficient special case. A direct inference from small smooth **mean** to higher centered moments is inadequate, including the source’s displayed multiplicative shortcut, which is unnecessary under the additive argument. The independent repair keeps the original range and does not replace its Fourier localization or historical role.
