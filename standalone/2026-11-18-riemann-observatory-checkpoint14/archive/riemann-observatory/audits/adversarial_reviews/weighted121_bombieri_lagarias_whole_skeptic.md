## Multiset criterion and convergence

Write \(w_\rho=\rho/(\rho-1)\), with \(w_0=0\). Theorem 1 excludes \(1\) and requires \(\sum_\rho (1+|\Re\rho|)/(1+|\rho|)^2<\infty\). Multiplicities are positive integers and enter every sum. This forces only finitely many occurrences on any compact subset of \(\mathbb C\). It does not force finitely many occurrences in a horizontal strip. Under the displayed summability, however, on each fixed height strip all sufficiently large \(|\rho|\) have \(|\Re\rho|\asymp|\rho|\). The complex sums for that strip converge absolutely there by comparison with \(\sum1/|\rho|\) restricted to the strip.

For a fixed integer \(n\), away from the finitely many small \(\rho\) and permitted denominator exceptions, \(1-(1-1/\rho)^n=n/\rho+O_n(|\rho|^{-2})\). This proves Lemma 1's absolute convergence for real parts, as \(|\Re(1/\rho)|=|\Re\rho|/|\rho|^2\). It also proves complex starred convergence when \(\sum^*1/\rho\) exists. Conjugation with identical multiplicity makes the latter sum real and starred convergent under the displayed hypothesis, since the height cutoff preserves conjugate pairs. Theorem 1 itself needs no conjugation or reflection. For \(n>0\) it uses only \(\sum\Re(1-w_\rho^n)\), where \(\rho=0\) contributes one. The \(n=0\) case of Lemma 1 is read as the constant-power identity with zero summands. The function \(w_\rho\) is defined at zero and only has the excluded pole at one.

For all \(\rho\ne1\),
\[
 |w_\rho|^2=1+\frac{2\Re\rho-1}{|\rho-1|^2}.
\]
Thus the left half-plane implies each real summand is nonnegative and implies both lower-bound conditions. Conversely, any right-half-plane point forces a maximum \(R>1\) of \(|w_\rho|\), attained at finitely many occurrences. Indeed \(|w_\rho|\to1\) as \(|\rho|\to\infty\) and there are only finitely many occurrences in a bounded disk. There is a uniform bound \(R_1\in(1,R)\) for the nonmaximal moduli. For \(|\rho|\le n\) the number of occurrences is \(O(n^2)\). For \(|\rho|>n\), expanding \(-n\log(1-1/\rho)\) bounds each real remainder by \(O((n|\Re\rho|+n^2)/|\rho|^2)\), uniformly for large \(n\), and its sum is \(O(n^2)\). Hence if the maximal arguments are \(\phi_j\), including repeats, the sum is
\[
 J-R^n\sum_{j=1}^J\cos(n\phi_j)+O(n^2R_1^n).
\]
Simultaneous approximation gives arbitrarily large positive \(n\) with all cosines close to one. The possible bounded-return case gives an exact integer common period and hence arbitrarily large multiples. The resulting negative values have magnitude at least \(cR^n\). Taking any \(0<\epsilon<\log R\) contradicts the required lower bound \(-c(\epsilon)e^{\epsilon n}\). This verifies necessity even when all extremal arguments are rational, or when a maximal zero has multiplicity.

Corollary 1 adds both symmetries as multiset symmetries and excludes 0 and 1. Reflection satisfies \((1-1/(1-\rho))=(1-1/\rho)^{-1}\) and preserves the height cutoff, so \(\lambda_n=\lambda_{-n}\). Conjugation makes the sums real. Reflection carries the one-sided half-plane to the full critical-line assertion. The general conclusion is **nonnegativity**, as needed for boundary multisets: \(R\) can be empty, and \(R=\{1/2\}\) has zero coefficients at all even indices. For Riemann zeros under RH the coefficients are strictly positive. The infinitely many unit-circle images tend to one and none equals one. For each fixed \(n\), some image is therefore not an \(n\)-th root of unity. A finite positive prefix is insufficient even under both multiset symmetries and summability: add an off-line symmetric quartet to sufficiently many copies of one on-line conjugate pair whose image angle divided by \(2\pi\) is irrational. This on-line pair contributes strictly positively at each prescribed index of the finite prefix, while the quartet still violates the line condition.

The remark weakening Corollary 1 to \(\sum_\rho(1+|\rho|)^{-2}<\infty\) is valid if one first groups reflection pairs, or if the height strips contain finitely many occurrences. In fact \(1/\rho+1/(1-\rho)=1/(\rho(1-\rho))\), so the paired real and complex sums converge absolutely after grouping, and the same \(O(n^2)\) large-\(|\rho|\) remainder argument applies to pairs because their linear terms cancel to order \(|\rho|^{-2}\). Convergence under the old literal height rule alone needs this qualification for general multisets. For example \(R=\{m+2,-m-1:m\ge1\}\) obeys the relaxed condition, is real and reflection invariant, and excludes 0 and 1. Already \(n=1\) gives divergent separate positive and negative parts in every height strip. Whole reflection-paired sums still converge. This is an interpretation issue in the relaxation remark with an explicit boundary example, rather than a counterexample to Theorem 1 or Corollary 1 under their stated main hypotheses.

## Generating function and exact normalization

Equations (2.3)–(2.4) are explicitly formal for arbitrary \(R\). If \(0\notin R\) and \(\sum^*1/\rho\) exists, absolute quadratic tails give locally uniform convergence of the symmetric genus-zero product by expanding \(\log(1-s/\rho)\), with a finite initial part treated separately. Since \(1\notin R\), the log derivative of \(f(1/(1-z))\) near \(z=0\) is holomorphic and its coefficients are \(\lambda_{-n-1}\) as printed. This follows factorwise from the ratio \(w_\rho\) or by holomorphic differentiation of the locally uniform product. The map \(s=(1-z)^{-1}\) carries \(|z|<1\) onto \(\Re s>1/2\). For a well-defined product the coefficient limsup at most one is equivalent to the absence of zeros from that open half-plane, since a zero gives a genuine pole of the log derivative. A finite multiplicity of zero, allowed in Theorem 1, could be accounted for by a factor \(s^m\), contributing \(m/(1-z)\) to the derivative. The authors' direct proof needs none of these product hypotheses.

Their \(\xi(s)=\frac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s)\) has \(\xi(0)=\xi(1)=1/2\); its zeros are exactly the nontrivial zeros with their multiplicities. To check the product normalization independently, let \(B=\xi'(0)/\xi(0)\) in the genus-one Hadamard product. At \(s=1\), the log derivative has value \(B+\sum(1/(1-\rho)+1/\rho)\), an absolutely convergent paired-denominator sum. Reflection gives that value \(-B\), and symmetric cutoff makes the sum \(2\sum^*1/\rho\). Thus \(B=-\sum^*1/\rho\) and the symmetric genus-zero product is \(2\xi(s)\). The constant has no effect on any Li derivative. The derivative definition is \(n[z^n]\log\xi((1-z)^{-1})\), by the substitution \(s-1=z/(1-z)\) in the Cauchy coefficient for \(s^{n-1}\log\xi(s)\). Consequently the positive-power formula in the introduction is the reflected expression of the inverse-power log derivative. Usual zero counting gives \(\sum(1+|\rho|)^{-2}<\infty\), and the strip bound on real parts gives the stronger summability here.

## Arithmetic and Weil comparison

The paper uses \(\widetilde f(x)=x^{-1}f(1/x)\) and \(\widehat f(s)=\int_0^\infty f(x)x^{s-1}\,dx\). Boundary conditions (A) and (B) are on pp. 5–6. Mellin inversion at a jump takes the midpoint. The gamma kernel in the explicit formula follows by folding the intervals at one and subtracting \(2f(1)/x^2\). For fixed \(\lambda\), the folded regularization with weight \(1-x^{-\lambda}\) is (3.3). The reference integral multiplying the subtracted constant is \(\frac12[\log(\lambda/2)+\gamma]+o(1)\), by substituting \(u=x^{-2}\) or summing \(1/(2j)\) when \(\lambda=2N\). This produces exactly the final \(-(\log\pi+\gamma) f(1)\) and the archimedean integral on p. 6. My use of the explicit formula is for its compact piecewise smooth inputs in the proof. The reference to Weil gives the upstream general theorem. I did not independently establish the most general unbounded-support test class in this audit.

For \(g_n(x)=P_n(\log x)\) on \(0<x<1\), with midpoint \(n/2\) at 1, its Mellin transform is \(1-(1-1/s)^n\) on \(\Re s>0\). Integrating logarithmic powers yields \((-1)^{j-1}(j-1)!/s^j\), which gives the correct binomial signs. This polynomial in \(\log x\) does not have condition (B) near zero, and the proof correctly starts with the compact cutoff \(g_{n,\epsilon}\) with a half endpoint at \(x=\epsilon\). For the prime sums the exact midpoint difference from a full endpoint is \(O(\epsilon(\log(1/\epsilon))^n)\) if \(1/\epsilon\) is integral, so it vanishes. The first two smooth terms give the subtraction \((\log(1/\epsilon))^j/j\) for each prime sum and the limit 1 from \(\int_0^1g_n(x)\,dx\). The archimedean calculation after \(x\mapsto1/x\) uses \(\sum_{m\ge0}(2m+1)^{-j}=(1-2^{-j})\zeta(j)\) for \(j\ge2\). The \(j=1\) part is \(n\log2\). These signs and constants give
\[
 \lambda_n=1-\frac n2(\log(4\pi)+\gamma)
            -\sum_{j=1}^n {n\choose j}\eta_{j-1}
            +\sum_{j=2}^n(-1)^j{n\choose j}(1-2^{-j})\zeta(j).
\]
Here \(\eta_j\) is the normalized prime-limit constant in (4.1). Partial summation against the classical PNT remainder makes every one of these finite and identifies them with the Laurent coefficients of \(-\zeta'/\zeta(1+s)-1/s\). Integration gives the series for \(\log(s\zeta(1+s))\) on p. 11. The manuscript's \(\gamma_j\) include \((-1)^j/j!\), as needed in its own Laurent convention. Independently, expand \(\log\xi(1+w)\) in \(w=z/(1-z)\). The \(\log(1+w)\) term contributes 1, the integrated Laurent expansion gives the \(\eta\) sum, and the derivatives of \(\log\Gamma((1+w)/2)\) at \(w=0\) give the odd-denominator zeta factors. This checks Theorem 2 without relying on a finite-part notation convention. At \(n=1\) it gives \(1+\gamma/2-\frac12\log(4\pi)\).

On the zero side, the Mellin cutoff error has leading term \(P_n(\log\epsilon)\epsilon^\rho/\rho\), and a remainder of size \(O_n(\epsilon^{\Re\rho}|\rho|^{-2}(1+\log(1/\epsilon))^{n-2})\) for \(n\ge2\). The classical zero-free region and reflection give \(\Re \rho\ge c/\log(2+|\rho|)\). Combine \(\epsilon^{\Re\rho}|\rho|^{-1/2}\ll\exp(-c'\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)})\) with \(\sum|\rho|^{-3/2}<\infty\) for the remainder. For the linear term reflect \(\rho\mapsto1-\rho\), replace \(1/(1-\rho)\) by \(-1/\rho+O(|\rho|^{-2})\), and invoke the classical explicit formula for the star sum of \((1/\epsilon)^\rho/\rho\), at an integer with the standard midpoint convention. The PNT with its classical error term then cancels the \(1/\epsilon\) main term. This verifies (3.8) with a margin over the fixed log powers.

In §4 the Mellin transform of \(f*\widetilde{\bar f}\) is \(\widehat f(s)\overline{\widehat f(1-\bar s)}\). On the line it is nonnegative. With \(A=(1-1/s)^n\) the reflected factor is \(A^{-1}\), so \((1-A)(1-A^{-1})=2-A-A^{-1}\). Mellin inversion yields the claimed \(g_n*\widetilde g_n=g_n+\widetilde g_n\), with common strip \(0<\Re s<1\). In this special family the spectral quadratic form is absolutely convergent because each large-\(|\rho|\) factor is \(O_n(|\rho|^{-1})\), and reflection gives the sum \(2\lambda_n\). To interpret the Weil comparison through compact cutoffs, the same expansion as in §3 bounds each truncated Mellin factor at zeros by \(O_n((1+\log(2+|\rho|))^{n-1}/|\rho|)\) uniformly in \(\epsilon\), using the zero-free region to bound \(\epsilon^{\Re\rho}|\log\epsilon|^j\). Thus the cutoff quadratic sums are dominated by an absolutely summable majorant. The positivity direction and Weil sufficiency have different inputs. The latter is credited to Weil; Corollary 1 supplies the sufficiency of the much smaller Li test family. Guinand's 1942 and 1948 papers play explicitly described precursor roles, and Li 1997 is the criterion being extended.

## Local display checks and bounded version search

- Manuscript p. 4 prints \(-n\) on \(1+t-\delta\) in the prose estimate for the nonmaximal terms, while the immediately following summed display prints \(+n\). The calculation above verifies the latter. The sign in the prose does not change the supported theorem.
- In (3.2), the generic finite-part notation \(2a(1)\log\lambda\) needs a meaning for the coefficient of the singular integrand. Ordinary substitution of \(a(x)=f(x)\max(1,x)/|x-1/x|\) makes \(a(1)\) undefined. The combined singular coefficient of that integral is \(f(1)\), since the two one-sided coefficients are \(f(1\pm)/2\). The fold and (3.4)–(3.7) compute with the correct subtraction \(f(1)\log\lambda\). This is a local notation ambiguity, with the intended regularization fixed by the actual computation.
- The exact equality in (3.5) stops the archimedean integral at \(1/\epsilon\). Only the \(g_{n,\epsilon}(1/x)\) summand vanishes after that point. The subtracted normalization contributes the omitted tail \(-\frac n2\log(1-\epsilon^2)\) to the full right-hand side. It tends to zero, so the subsequent limit and Theorem 2 are unchanged.
