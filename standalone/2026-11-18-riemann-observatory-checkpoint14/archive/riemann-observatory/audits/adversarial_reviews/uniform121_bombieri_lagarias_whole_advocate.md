## Pages 1–5 — sums, criterion and the two converse routes

Let \(\mathcal R\) be a multiset with finite positive integer multiplicities at each point and
\[
 \sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R}\frac{1+|\Re\rho|}{(1+|\rho|)^2}<\infty.
\]
In particular every bounded disk contains finitely many points with multiplicity. The height-symmetric sum uses \(|\Im\rho|\le T\) before taking \(T\to\infty\). Lemma 1 excludes \(0\) for a positive power \((1-1/\rho)^n\), and excludes \(1\) for a negative power. At \(\rho=0\) the inverse-power expression is interpreted as \((\rho/(\rho-1))^n=0\) for positive \(n\), so the half-plane theorem does admit zero. The large-\(|\rho|\) expansion of \(1-(1-1/\rho)^n\) is \(n/\rho+O_n(|\rho|^{-2})\). Its real-part terms are absolutely summable. The complex sum requires the separate height-symmetric summability of \(\sum 1/\rho\), supplied by conjugation symmetry when zero is excluded. One can form each fixed-height sum under the stronger hypothesis even if the strip is horizontally unbounded, since large \(|\Re\rho|\) there makes \(1/|\rho|\) summable by the same weight. This does not imply absolute summability of the individual full complex summands across all heights.

With \(1\notin\mathcal R\), Theorem 1 states the equivalence between all \(\Re\rho\le1/2\), nonnegativity of
\[
 S_n=\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal R}\Re\,[1-(\rho/(\rho-1))^n]
\]
for every positive integer \(n\), and for every fixed \(\epsilon>0\) a constant \(c(\epsilon)\) making \(S_n\ge-c(\epsilon)e^{\epsilon n}\) for every \(n\). The norm identity \(|\rho/(\rho-1)|^2=1+(2\Re\rho-1)/|1-\rho|^2\) proves the forward implication term by term. If any point lies to the right of one half, the maximal modulus \(R>1\) is attained by finitely many points. All other moduli are bounded by a fixed \(R_0\in[1,R)\), since the ratios tend to 1 as \(|\rho|\to\infty\). For \(|\rho|\le2n\) local finiteness and the weight bound give \(O(n^2)\) terms. For \(|\rho|>2n\), expanding \(\exp(-n\log(1-1/\rho))\) through its linear term gives a real-part bound \(O((n|\Re\rho|+n^2)/|\rho|^2)\), summable with an \(O(n^2)\) total. Hence the maximal points contribute \(K-R^n\sum_{j=1}^K\cos(n\phi_j)\), with the remainder \(O(n^2R_0^n)\). Dirichlet's simultaneous approximation gives arbitrarily large \(n\) with all cosines near 1. To see the unboundedness, either the approximating denominators are unbounded or a bounded repeated denominator is an exact common return and its multiples work. Along that sequence \(S_n\le-cR^n\), disproving the subexponential lower bound for any \(0<\epsilon<\log R\). This is the actual proof and requires no product representation.

The off-line pole route on p. 3 is a formal remark when the product \(f(s)=\prod(1-s/\rho)\) is well defined. The map \(s=(1-z)^{-1}\) and the logarithmic derivative give coefficients \(\lambda_{-n-1}\) in degree \(n\), so a right-half-plane zero gives a disk pole. Pure radius-of-convergence reasoning alone controls absolute coefficient growth and does not prove the one-sided lower-bound converse. If one wants an analytic substitute, form the real-coefficient generating function directly from \(w=\rho/(\rho-1)\), using
\[
 \frac1{1-z}-\frac12\left(\frac{w}{1-wz}+\frac{\bar w}{1-\bar w z}\right)
\]
for each point, in degree \(n-1\). Its first-order variation at \(w=1\) involves only \(\Re(w-1)\), and the remaining term is \(O(|w-1|^2)\) locally away from \(z=1\) and the discrete poles. The weight assumption gives normal convergence there. The smallest-modulus poles have radius \(R^{-1}<1\) and all residues are positive multiples of \(1/2\) at their locations, so there is no cancellation. Add a geometric series for \(c(\epsilon)e^{\epsilon n}\) with \(\epsilon<\log R\) if the lower bound holds. The shifted series has nonnegative coefficients and the same radius. Pringsheim's theorem forces a positive real singularity there. If present, its positive-residue pole makes the series tend to negative infinity from below along the real radius, incompatible with its nonnegative coefficients. This supplementary route is not attributed as the author's printed proof.

Corollary 1 adds exclusion of \(0,1\) and exact reflection \(\rho\mapsto1-\rho\) and conjugation symmetry with multiplicity. Reflection preserves the height cutoff and replaces the Cayley factor by its reciprocal, so the positive-power and inverse-power sums agree after summing. Reflection also turns the half-plane conclusion into the critical-line conclusion. The weaker inverse-square hypothesis in the p. 5 remark is valid with orbit grouping in each height cutoff. The reflection orbit cancels the first-order expansion since \(1/\rho+1/(1-\rho)=1/[\rho(1-\rho)]\); conjugation makes the result real. The same maximal-modulus proof works for this grouped tail with \(O(n^2/|\rho|^2)\) beyond radius \(2n\) up to a fixed expansion margin. Under this weaker hypothesis one must group before summing within a strip when infinitely many points lie there. The real multiset formed by the orbits of integers \(j\ge2\) and their reflections illustrates that an arbitrary order of ungrouped terms need not converge.

## Pages 5–10 — explicit formula, hypothesis match and cutoff identity

The manuscript uses \(\widetilde f(x)=x^{-1}f(1/x)\) and \(\widehat f(s)=\int_0^\infty f(x)x^{s-1}\,dx\). It derives its Mellin formula from Weil by setting \(F(t)=e^{t/2}f(e^t)\). The stated class (A) has finitely many first-kind jumps for the function and derivative and assigns averaged values at jumps. Condition (B) requires \(f(x)=O(x^\delta)\) near zero and \(O(x^{-1-\delta})\) at infinity. The cited Weil condition, however, bounds **both** \(F\) and \(F'\) by \(O(e^{-(1/2+\delta)|t|})\). For example, a smooth cutoff of \(x^{-2}\sin(x^3)\), equal to that function for \(x\ge2\) and zero near zero, satisfies the manuscript's printed class but violates Weil's derivative bound. This establishes a source-hypothesis mismatch, not by itself a counterexample to the general explicit formula. A sufficient direct match adds \(f'(x)=O(x^{\delta-1})\) and \(O(x^{-2-\delta})\) at the respective ends, possibly with a smaller positive \(\delta\). The actual compact piecewise-smooth truncated tests in Theorem 2 meet this. [Weil 1952 primary work, transcription of conditions (A), (B) and the finite-part definition](https://denisevellachemla.eu/transc-Weil-1952.pdf)

The logarithmic finite part also fixes the reading of (3.2). The kernel written as \(a(x)\) is singular at 1. In Weil's logarithmic coordinate the coefficient is the mean one-sided value of \(|t|a(e^t)\), which for this kernel is \(f(1)/2\). The subtraction is therefore \(f(1)\log\lambda\), not a literal ordinary evaluation of the singular \(a\) at 1. After inversion on \((0,1)\), the kernel becomes \([f(x)+\widetilde f(x)]x/(x^2-1)\) on \((1,\infty)\). The compensation \(2f(1)/x^2\) leaves an integrable difference at 1. The normalization integral at \(\lambda=2N\) is \(H_N/2\) by a geometric sum, and monotonicity between consecutive even values gives \((\log(\lambda/2)+\gamma)/2+o(1)\). Together with the printed \(-\log 2\pi\) term this produces exactly \(- (\log\pi+\gamma)f(1)\) in the displayed explicit formula.

Lemma 2 inverts \(1-(1-1/s)^n\) on \(\Re s>0\), using \(\int_0^1 (\log x)^{j-1}x^{s-1}\,dx=(-1)^{j-1}(j-1)!/s^j\). The inverse is the stated polynomial of \(\log x\) below 1, half-value \(n/2\) at 1, zero above. The polynomial need not decay at zero, so restrict to \(x\ge\epsilon\) with a half-weight at \(\epsilon\). This compact test meets the matched Weil conditions. In the prime sum it gives a half-weight at \(m=1/\epsilon\) when this is an integer, a negligible \(O_n(\epsilon(\log(1/\epsilon))^n)\) change when taking the limit.

**Local exact error in (3.5), p. 8.** The printed last integral stops at \(1/\epsilon\), although its subtraction \(2g_{n,\epsilon}(1)/x^2=n/x^2\) remains nonzero beyond that point. The correct exact upper limit is infinity. Equivalently add the positive term
\[
 n\int_{1/\epsilon}^\infty\frac{dx}{x(x^2-1)}
 =-\frac n2\log(1-\epsilon^2)
]
to the printed right side. With \(n=1,\epsilon=2/3\), there is no prime endpoint, and the missing amount is exactly \(\tfrac12\log(9/5)>0\). This is an explicit contradiction to the finite identity within its allowed parameter range. The correction is \(O_n(\epsilon^2)\) and leaves the theorem's limit unchanged.

For that limit, the two elementary integrals yield the log-power terms in (3.6) and the constant 1 in (3.7). In the gamma term, the substitution \(x\mapsto1/x\) leaves \(\int_0^1[g_n(x)-nx]/(1-x^2)\,dx\). The linear part integrates to \(n\log2\). Expanding \((1-e^{-2t})^{-1}\) after \(x=e^{-t}\) supplies \((1-2^{-j})\zeta(j)\) with the displayed alternating binomial coefficients. The fixed-\(j\) prime limits exist by partial summation with the quantitative PNT, \(\psi(X)-X=O(Xe^{-c\sqrt{\log X}})\). One can also check the final coefficient formula directly by setting \(u=s-1=z/(1-z)\) and expanding
\[
 \log(2\xi(1+u))=\log(1+u)+\log(u\zeta(1+u))
      -\tfrac u2\log\pi+\log\Gamma((1+u)/2)-\log\Gamma(1/2).
\]
Indeed \(n[z^n]\log(1+u)=1\), the log-gamma linear coefficient gives \(-n(\gamma+\log 4\pi)/2\) after the \(\pi\) term, and the higher polygamma coefficients give the printed odd-integer zeta factors. The logarithmic derivative of \(u\zeta(1+u)\) gives \(-\sum_{j=1}^n\binom nj\eta_{j-1}\) in this coefficient formula. For \(n=1\), \(\eta_0=-\gamma\) yields \(1+\gamma/2-\tfrac12\log4\pi\).

The zero-side limiting step (3.8) is separate from the arithmetic cutoff. Integration by parts gives a leading \(P_n(\log\epsilon)\epsilon^s/s\) and an inverse-square remainder bounded by \(O_n(\epsilon^{\Re s}|s|^{-2}(\log(1/\epsilon))^{n-2})\), absent for \(n=1\). The zero-free region and reflection bound \(\sum_\rho \epsilon^{\Re\rho}|\rho|^{-2}\) by \(e^{-c'\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}}\) up to constants, using the maximum of \(e^{-cL/u-u/2}\), \(u=\log(|\rho|+2)\), against the summable inverse-three-halves series. The leading \(\sum_\rho\epsilon^\rho/\rho\) must be kept in its symmetric order. Reflect it, replace \((1-\rho)^{-1}\) by \(-1/\rho\) with the same inverse-square error, and use the classical explicit formula for \(\psi(1/\epsilon)\) and the quantitative PNT. Midpoint convention at a jump changes the scaled answer by a negligible amount. This justifies the limit without termwise absolute summation of the leading terms.

## Pages 10–12 — positivity, Li normalization and reference roles

Multiplicative convolution with measure \(dy/y\) multiplies Mellin transforms, and \(f*\widetilde{\bar f}\) has transform \(|\widehat f(s)|^2\) on the critical line. Positivity here is nonnegativity. The rational identity for \(g_n\) follows from \((1-1/s)(1-1/(1-s))=1\), so the sum of the two transforms equals their product. This identifies the Li tests with a particular Weil quadratic form, but the untruncated \(g_n\) does not meet the decay hypothesis. Use the already justified cutoff limit, or the absolutely summable paired zero side of the product, when making this comparison. It does not assert an equivalence between finite positivity tests and Weil's complete test family. The last Laurent formulas give \(\eta_n\) as polynomials in the Stieltjes constants under the source's normalization with the signs and factorials absorbed into \(\gamma_n\). For example, \(\eta_0=-\gamma_0\) and \(\eta_1=\gamma_0^2-2\gamma_1\) follow by differentiation of the displayed logarithmic series.

In the locally pinned Li journal manifestation, \(\Xi=2\xi\), so the constant in the local logarithm is killed by the \(n\)-th derivative of \(s^{n-1}\log\Xi(s)\). Thus both zero sums and derivative coefficients match. The nontrivial zeta and Dedekind zeros have bounded real parts, reflection and conjugation symmetries, and normal inverse-square summability, so the multiset corollary applies. Li's proof uses positivity of theta-kernel coefficients to continue the logarithmic derivative, unlike the general Diophantine proof. For the zeta zeros, under RH the nonnegative real-pair sums are in fact strictly positive at every fixed \(n\), since infinitely many distinct zeros cannot all map to the finitely many \(n\)-th roots of unity. Strict positivity would be false for a general on-line multiset, for example \(\{1/2\}\) at even \(n\). [Pinned Li and its edition-specific adjudication](director_li_whole_round12.md)
