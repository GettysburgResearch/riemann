## The equivalence

For integer \(n\), exchanging a finite sum with the integral gives
\[
\int_1^n\frac{\lfloor t\rfloor}{t^2}\,dt
=\sum_{j=1}^n\int_j^n\frac{dt}{t^2}=H_n-1.
\]
Consequently
\[
H_n=\log n+\gamma+r_n,\qquad
\gamma=1-\int_1^\infty\frac{\{t\}}{t^2}\,dt>0,\qquad
0<r_n=\int_n^\infty\frac{\{t\}}{t^2}\,dt<1/n.
\]
The limiting formula identifies \(\gamma\) with Euler's constant. The opening derivation on May p. 6 prints an infinite upper limit and the summand integral also has incorrect limits. The displayed (3.4) has the finite outer endpoint, and July p. 7 gives the finite calculation. For \(n\ge3\), both factors can be compared positively, giving \(e^{H_n}\log H_n>e^\gamma n\log\log n\) as in Lemma 3.1. [May p. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0008177v2#page=6) · [July p. 7](https://websites.umich.edu/lagarias/doc/elementaryrh.pdf#page=7)

For May Lemma 3.2, put \(x=\log n\). Then \(H_n\le x+1\), \(e^{H_n}\le e^\gamma n(1+2/n)\) by the exponential secant on \([0,1]\), and \(\log H_n\le\log x+1/x\). One has \(e^\gamma<2\) from \(\gamma<H_5-\log 5<\log 2\) since \(H_5=137/60<\log 10\). For \(x\ge2\), \(x\log x+1\le x^2/2+1\le e^x\). Hence the excess of the exponential product over the baseline is at most \(2n/x+4(\log x+1/x)\le6n/x\). Also \(x(x+1)\le e^x\) for \(x\ge1\): the cubic Taylor lower bound leaves \(1-x^2/2+x^3/6\), with minimum \(1/3\) at \(x=2\). Adding \(H_n\) gives the May \(7n/\log n\) bound for \(n\ge20\). The sharper intermediate log term printed in May (3.8) holds as well. For \(u=1/x\), integration gives \(\log(1+u)\le u-u^2/(2(1+u))\), while \(1/\log(n+1)\ge u-u^2/n\). Compare using \(n\ge2(1+u)\) for \(n\ge4\), with \(n=3\) a direct check. [May p. 7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0008177v2#page=7)

July Lemma 3.2 uses \(H_n-\log(n+1)\uparrow\gamma\), by summing \(1/j-\log(1+1/j)>0\). Thus \(e^{H_n}\le e^\gamma(n+1)\). On \(x\ge1\), \(e^x/2-x\log x-1>0\): it is positive at 1 and its derivative is at least \(e^x/2-x>0\), using \(\log x\le x-1\) and \(e^x/x\ge e\). Therefore \(\log x+1/x\le n/(2x)\). Multiplying \(e^\gamma(n+1)\) by \(\log x+1/x\) and adjoining \(H_n\) bounds the total excess by \((1+3e^\gamma/2)n/x<4n/x\) for \(n\ge3\). [July p. 8](https://websites.umich.edu/lagarias/doc/elementaryrh.pdf#page=8)

Robin's Theorem 1, and §3 Proposition 3, give under RH the strict bound \(\sigma(n)<e^\gamma n\log\log n\) for integers \(n>5040\). The harmonic lower bound finishes all these integers. A divisor-multiple sieve with exact integer sums, paired with 45-decimal directed interval accumulation of \(H_n\), exponentiation and logarithms, checks \(n=1,\ldots,5041\). Equality is direct at 1, and the smallest positive gap is at 2, greater than 0.3171685434. The same interval replay checks the exponential product alone for \(61\le n\le5041\) and finds a negative gap at 60. This is the finite portion of July's Kaneko assertion. [May proof](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0008177v2#page=8) · [July p. 9](https://websites.umich.edu/lagarias/doc/elementaryrh.pdf#page=9) · [Independent replay](../../scripts/geometry64_lagarias_whole_checks.py)

If RH fails, take a nontrivial zero with real part \(b>1/2\) and choose \(1-b<\beta<1/2\). Robin's §4, printed pp. 204–205, gives signed oscillations along CA integers for the permitted range \(1-\Theta<\beta<1/2\), where \(\Theta\) is the supremum of real parts of nontrivial zeros. Since \(\Theta\ge b\), the selected \(\beta\) applies. The positive side gives unbounded \(n\) with excess at least \(C n\log\log n/(\log n)^\beta\) for a fixed \(C>0\). Dividing by \(7n/\log n\) gives \((C/7)(\log n)^{1-\beta}\log\log n\to\infty\), and the same holds with the July constant 4. Thus Problem E fails along an unbounded CA subsequence, as does the version with the exponential product alone. This implication imports Robin's oscillation as published, whose ancestry includes Nicolas and Landau. The proof establishes existence without computing a first violating integer. ## Contextual results and checks

For §2, multiplication of the geometric sums over prime powers gives (2.1). Divisor switching gives \(\sum_{n\le X}\sigma(n)=\frac12\sum_{k\le X}\lfloor X/k\rfloor(\lfloor X/k\rfloor+1)=\zeta(2)X^2/2+O(X\log X)\). For maximal order, split prime divisors at \(\log n\). The large-prime factors contribute \(\exp(O(1/\log\log n))\) since their count is at most \(\log n/\log\log n\). The inverse Mertens product on the small primes supplies \(e^\gamma\log\log n\). For the lower bound use \(n=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,\lfloor X\rfloor)\). Here \(\log n=\psi(X)\sim X\). Numerator Euler factors approach 1 since their losses total \(O(X^{-1/2})+\sum_{p>\sqrt X}p^{-2}=o(1)\). The printed ordinary Mertens product in both versions has \(e^\gamma/\log X\), with the correct constant \(e^{-\gamma}\). Both versions also print Robin's strict (2.2) with coefficient 0.6482. At \(n=12\), equality forces coefficient \((7/3-e^\gamma\log\log12)\log\log12\in(0.64821364942179,0.64821364942181)\). Robin's original sharp coefficient gives equality there. The strict bound with 0.6483 is valid. The asserted scale comparison is asymptotic, since \(n/\log n=o(n/\log\log n)\). [May p. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0008177v2#page=3) · [July p. 3](https://websites.umich.edu/lagarias/doc/elementaryrh.pdf#page=3)

A CA maximum separates into local factors. The exponent may step from \(a-1\) to \(a\) at the threshold
\[
\epsilon_{p,a}=\frac{\log(1+1/(p+\cdots+p^a))}{\log p}.
\]
This is strictly decreasing in \(p\) and in \(a\). The step condition is \(p^{a+1}\le(p^{1+\epsilon}-1)/(p^\epsilon-1)\), which gives (2.3) and the larger exponent at an equality. Each tied prime independently permits its smaller exponent too. The generic maxima are unique, and positive thresholds are locally finite away from zero. With equality ties, the Alaoglu–Erdős smaller-maximizer convention selects a single minimum at a breakpoint. For \(m<n\), CA maximization yields \(\sigma(n)/n\ge(n/m)^\epsilon\sigma(m)/m>\sigma(m)/m\), proving CA implies superabundant. Infinitely many primes have a positive first threshold and all enter eventually as \(\epsilon\downarrow0\). The same strict comparison with \(d\) gives the superior highly composite implication under the usual optimization over positive integers. The printed \(k>1\) comparisons are read with the accompanying “all” maximization definitions. Under orthodox CA maximization, 1 is also a maximizer at large \(\epsilon\); Table 1 lists the nontrivial entries.

For the table, I ordered the first 23 positive thresholds using disjoint intervals. For \(p>41\), the first threshold is below \(\epsilon_{41,1}\); for omitted \(a\ge16\), \(\epsilon_{2,16}<\epsilon_{41,1}\). These facts certify the scope of enumeration. All 22 displayed nontrivial integers and factorizations match, as do their ratios truncated to three decimal places by exact rational arithmetic. The next integer is \(9200527969062830400>10^{18}\). The paper's 1, 2 or 4 multiplicity statement relies on the Erdős–Nicolas result p. 70. The four-exponentials conjecture, applied to \(\log p,\log q\) and \(1,x\), rules out irrational \(x\) with rational \(p^x,q^x\) for distinct primes. A reduced rational \(x\) must be integral by prime valuation. Thus the conjecture would rule out simultaneous prime thresholds at \(x=\epsilon\). Each consecutive CA quotient would then be a prime, and each maximum would occur throughout some open parameter interval. ### Colossally abundant endpoint convention

May p. 4 footnote 1 and July p. 5 footnote 1 identify the Alaoglu–Erdős strict smaller-integer requirement with the numbers parametrized by (2.3). Pointwise in \(\epsilon\), that identification needs an endpoint qualification. For \(\epsilon=\log(4/3)/\log 3=\epsilon_{3,1}\), the threshold comparison is \(\epsilon_{2,1}>\epsilon_{3,1}>\epsilon_{2,2},\epsilon_{5,1}\). Monotonicity leaves exactly \(2\) and \(6\) as maximizers, and their normalized objectives coincide because \(3^\epsilon=4/3\). Formula (2.3) takes exponent one for 3 and selects 6. The strict inequality for every smaller integer selects 2 at that parameter. In general the smaller-maximizer convention replaces \(\lfloor x\rfloor-1\) by \(\lceil x\rceil-2\) at a positive integral breakpoint \(x=\log_p((p^{1+\epsilon}-1)/(p^\epsilon-1))\). The footnote is defensible as a statement about the set of all values attained as \(\epsilon\) varies. The minimum at a tied parameter is the generic maximum immediately above it, and the maximum from (2.3) is the generic maximum immediately below it, by local finiteness of thresholds. These one-sided statements also hold when two distinct primes tie. The pointwise distinction leaves the equivalence unchanged.
