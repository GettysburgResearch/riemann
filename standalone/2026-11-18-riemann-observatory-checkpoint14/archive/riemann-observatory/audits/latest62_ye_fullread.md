# Ye v2 short-strip density and its applications

17 November 2026. Full reading of Yangbo Ye, arXiv:2610.15989v2, PDF pp. 1–26, including all proofs in §§2–6, the tables and references. There is no appendix. This is a substantive Level 1 reading of the preprint and a reconstruction of the connection below. The source's cited inputs were not each audited in full.

Theorem 1.1 assumes a uniform-in-start, short critical-line \(2\ell\)-moment of exponent \(\theta\ge a\) in intervals of length \(T^a\), for fixed \(0<a\le1\) and \(\ell\ge1\). It applies to zeta or a fixed globally tempered, unitary cuspidal \(\pi\) of \(\mathrm{GL}_d\) over a fixed number field. Zeros are counted with multiplicities, inclusive ordinate bounds, and strict \(\Re\rho>\sigma\). The claimed range is \(3/5\le\sigma<1\). Constants depend on the representation and fixed parameters.

## Detector and exact balance

For a detector cutoff \(X=T^\kappa\) chosen with sufficiently small fixed \(\kappa>0\), the inverse-truncated Euler convolution \(b_X\) vanishes for \(2\le n\le X\) and is subpower after fixing all convolution orders. At a zero with \(\Re\rho>\sigma\), Mellin inversion using \(e^{-n/Y}\) produces either a large dyadic polynomial (Type I) or a large critical-line integral (Type II). The gamma truncation needs only an \(O((\log T)^2)\) enlargement, and bounded covering uses the uniform-in-start moment. Hölder and the unit-window zero multiplicity bound give \(R_{\rm II}\ll T^{\theta+\varepsilon}Y^{-\ell(2\sigma-1)}\).

For Type I, use (2.9) to move to \(\Re s=\sigma\) via a Fourier convolution in log-length. Displacing ordinates by at most \(T^\eta\) costs \(T^\eta\log T\) when thinning to 1-separated samples, but allows the large-value height to be \(O(H)\), \(H=T^a\), once \(\eta<a/2\). The independent scale \(Z\ge Y^2\) permits bounded powering of a block of size \(M\) so the new block length satisfies \(Z^{2/3}\ll N\ll Z(\log T)^4\). The translated polynomial has coefficients of absolute value at most 1 and sample threshold \(N^\sigma T^{-\eta}\), after selecting the smaller coefficient losses once the powering indices are fixed. The Guth–Maynard and ordinary mean-value bounds have respectively the additional powers \(N^{18/5-4\sigma}+HN^{12/5-4\sigma}\) and \(HN^{1-2\sigma}\), sharing \(N^{2(1-\sigma)}\). On \(3/5\le\sigma\le4/5\), split at \(N=Z^{5(1-\sigma)/(9-10\sigma)}\) and use (2.15) for the long side; for \(\sigma\ge4/5\) the middle term is absorbed directly. The resulting \(Z\)-bound has terms \(Z^{2(1-\sigma)}+HZ^{8/5-8\sigma/3}\). At \(Z=\max(Y^2,H^{15/(2(3+5\sigma))})\) the second is bounded by the first. This recovers the independently determined Type I exponent and explains why increasing the critical-line moment does not affect it.

Set \(b=\ell(2\sigma-1),c=4(1-\sigma)\) at this detector step. Taking \(Y=T^{\theta/(b+c)}\) balances Type II with \(Y^c\). The final local exponent is
\[
 (1-\sigma)\max\left\{\frac{15a}{3+5\sigma},
 \frac{4\theta}{\ell(2\sigma-1)+4(1-\sigma)}\right\}+\varepsilon .
\]
Theorem 1.2 takes \((\ell,\theta)=(2,a)\) for \(a\ge2/3\) and intersects the result with Ye–Zhang's estimates. Its middle segment \(15a(1-\sigma)/(3+5\sigma)\) operates on \([7/10,4/5]\). The other entries are \(3a(1-\sigma)/(2-\sigma)\), \(3a(1-\sigma)/(3\sigma-1)\) and \(2a(1-\sigma)\) on the stated adjacent bands. Only \(a=1\) is directly comparable with \(T\)-scale global counts. This density argument has no fixed zero-free half-plane assumption.

## Oscillatory applications and proof details

For \(m\le K^R\), let \(u=\log m/\log K,\ b=\beta+u,\ v=b+\delta-1,\ w_0=1-\delta\). The explicit formula uses the logarithmic-variable Fourier transform of \(G(y)=e^{y/2}w(e^y)\), so off-critical zero transforms retain \(x^{\sigma-1}\). \(G\) has positive log-support, eliminating the dual prime and conductor terms. The digamma integral yields the exact gamma contribution from \(-w(x)x^{-1-\mu_j}/(1-x^{-2})\), and all pole and gamma terms decay to arbitrary order in \(K^{v}\) by nonstationary integration. The mixed transform bound is \(K^\sigma\min(K^{-w_0},K^{-b/2})\). The two widths of the zero window are \(K^v\) and \(K^{w_0+\eta}\). In rescaled log-coordinate the off-window derivative is bounded below by a fixed multiple of \(mK^\beta(L/K)^2+(L/K)\operatorname{dist}(t,I_m)\), using the gap between the support and the integration interval. This drives rapid decay even when curvature is small, after extending the Fourier width. If \(\delta>1-\beta/2\), curvature is a positive power and the stationary window alone suffices. The page image confirms that Proposition 3.3 uses the same-height symmetry \(\rho\mapsto1-\bar\rho\).

In §4 zeta uses the additional \(7/8\) zero-free boundary cited by Ye. Partial summation of \(\sum K^{\Re\rho}\) adds the exact local total count at \(1/2\) and the integral of the strict upper counts up to \(7/8\), retaining endpoint zeros. Insert \(\nu=\max(v,w_0,2b/3)\); optimization of \(\sigma+\nu B(\sigma)\) gives the four pieces of \(Q\) and produces (1.11). Section 4 separately treats the earlier Ye–Zhang envelope, general fixed boundary \(\vartheta\in[6/7,11/12]\), and the allowable frequencies via \(E(u)-E(0)\le u/2\). The fixed real-part cutoff matters to the upper-end weighted maximum and does not improve the detector itself.

Sections 5–6 treat fixed self-dual forms and do not use Theorem 1.1 for their density inputs. The GL(2) twist has fixed real primitive \(\chi\), \(N\mid Q\), and uses Ye–Zhang plus the local total zero count. Maximizing the lower-band exponent gives the truncated stationary point \(\min\{3/4,\max\{(b+v)/(b+2v),2-\sqrt{b+3v}\}\}\); above \(3/4\), convexity reduces to endpoints. In the derivative check for Corollary 1.2 the upper comparison is for \(\sqrt C\le2-c<4/3\), with \(C=b+3v\) and \(c=(b+v)/(b+2v)>2/3\). The page image confirms that the square root is present; the needed derivative is \(3-3/\sqrt C<1\). The GL(3) case likewise uses earlier density estimates, and its entire effective envelope simplifies to \(\min(v,2(b+v)(1-\sigma))\). The two endpoints producing Theorem 1.5 then come from a linear maximum. Neither application assumes Ramanujan. Their frequency correction comes from increasing \(b,v\) by \(u\), which costs at most \(u\) in the density envelope and gains \(u/2\) in the transform.

## A direct shorter-height envelope

For every fixed \(0<a\le1\), the very same short fourth moment (2.17) gives \(\theta=\max(a,2/3)\) in Theorem 1.1, even when \(a<2/3\), by extending each interval at the same start. Thus
\[
 N_\zeta(\sigma;T,T+T^a)\ll_{a,\varepsilon}
 T^{(1-\sigma)\max\{15a/(3+5\sigma),\,2\max(a,2/3)\}+\varepsilon},
 \qquad 3/5\le\sigma<1 .
\]
At \(\sigma=3/4,\ a=3/5\) this is \(T^{1/3+\varepsilon}\), versus \(T^{10/27+\varepsilon}\) from simply covering with the \(a=2/3\) middle branch of Theorem 1.2. These compare two implementations in the same paper. There is no assertion about the best available estimate. Global higher moments can be inserted in Type II but must keep their global exponent on the smaller window. For instance \(\ell=3,\theta=37/30\) from the global sixth moment gives Type II \(4(37/30)/(2\sigma+1)\), which beats the fourth-moment \(2a\) branch for \(a\ge2/3\) only if \(37/30<a(\sigma+1/2)\). For \(\sigma\le9/10\) and \(a\ge2/3\), \(15a/(3+5\sigma)\ge2a\), so this substitution cannot improve the detector in that band. A sharper Type I estimate or a distinct detector would be needed to exploit further moment gains there.

A further consequence of the shorter-height envelope is a small improvement in the same absolute-value argument for \(m=1,\ \beta=3/5,\ \delta=7/10\). Here \(q=w_0=v=3/10\), \(b=3/5\), and the transform costs \(K^{-3/10}\). Enlarge the height window by the permitted small margin and take \(a=1/2+o_\varepsilon(1)\); the Type I slope is at most \(5/4+o_\varepsilon(1)<4/3\) for \(\sigma\ge3/5\). Its density bound in the \(K\)-variable is \(K^{(4/5)(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}\). Below \(\sigma=3/5\), the trivial local count contributes at most \(K^{9/10+\varepsilon}\) to the weighted sum. Above \(3/5\) the exponent \(\sigma+(4/5)(1-\sigma)\) increases to \(39/40\) at the paper's cited \(7/8\) cutoff. Applying Proposition 3.3 gives \(O(K^{27/40+\varepsilon})\), compared with \(O(K^{44/65+\varepsilon})\) from (1.11) at the same parameters. The cutoff is a separate hypothesis of this derived comparison, and no independent proof of the cutoff is claimed here.

## Weighted BGSTB height slice

Write \(y=(\Re\rho-1/2)\log T\) and give each zero the weight \(e^{2y}/(1+y^2)\). For any single uniform window of length \(T^{3/5}\) at heights \(\asymp T\), the contribution from \(\tfrac12\le\Re\rho\le1-d\) is \(o(T)\) for each fixed \(0<d<2/5\). Below \(3/5\), the trivial local count gives \(O(T^{4/5+\varepsilon})\). On \([3/5,1-d]\), the shorter-height density slope is at most \(3/2\), so the count times the weight at the same threshold is \(O(T^{1-(1-\sigma)/2+\varepsilon})\). Partial summation, or bins of width \(1/\log T\), shows the weighted contribution is \(O_{d,\varepsilon}(T^{1-d/2+\varepsilon})\). A strict real-part threshold can be shifted left by \(1/\log T\) for inclusive counts. Near the \(3/5\) junction use the trivial estimate on the resulting \(O(1/\log T)\) overlap. This controls a real-height slice, including its moving critical boundary. It does not supply global BGSTB tightness. Covering a full dyadic height block uses \(O(T^{2/5})\) such windows, which spoils the power savings. In particular the local density slopes do not force the microscopic \(o(T)\) boundary count summed over the full block. The fixed real-part strip excluded by \(d\) would need separate estimates as well unless a fixed zero-free boundary is assumed.
