## Completeness and numerical enclosure

A certified opposite sign for Hardy's real-valued function at the ends of each of several disjoint brackets supplies a lower bound on the number of critical-line zeros. Counting all strip zeros with multiplicity is a separate step. In the normalization \(N(t)=1+\theta(t)/\pi+S(t)\) away from zero ordinates, a deficit of at least one in a candidate count raises the integral of \(S\) over any whole test interval on which that deficit persists. Turing's method uses an explicit bound for this integrated argument with enough extra evaluations to certify the endpoint count. If the disjoint brackets account for the whole count with multiplicity, each bracket contains one simple zero and there is no leftover strip zero in the tested range. Büthe's separate criterion bounds an explicit-formula residual instead. Both have finite ordinate domains. Density estimates concern asymptotic counts in specified parts of the strip. [Turing's original article](https://doi.org/10.1112/plms/s3-3.1.99) · [Trudgian's 2011 paper](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-2011-02470-1) · [Büthe's criterion](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-2015-02922-6)

The early numerical papers report the 75-million, 200,000,001, 300,000,001 and 1,500,000,001 initial censuses. The printed corrigenda for Parts II and III change the classification of Rosser-rule exceptions and related counts. Part IV used five additional Gram blocks of lengths \(1,1,2,1,1\), spanning six Gram intervals. Turing's 1953 paper gives its own caution about the reliability of the high-band run. The 1943 date printed on Turing's earlier offprint differs from the 1945 date on the DOI landing page, so the records preserve both date conventions. [Brent's first census](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1979-0537983-2) · [Part II and corrigendum](https://maths-people.anu.edu.au/~brent/pub/pub070.html) · [Part IV](https://ir.cwi.nl/pub/1808)

Platt's 2017 work combines explicit truncation bounds, MPFI interval enclosures and completeness counting. It reports every nontrivial zero through ordinate \(30{,}610{,}046{,}000\), with absolute isolation precision \(\pm 2^{-102}\). The later Platt–Trudgian computation uses Arb for the enclosures, keeps the precision needed to certify a count, and reaches \(H=3{,}000{,}175{,}332{,}800\) with \(12{,}363{,}153{,}437{,}138\) zeros accounted for. The paper and its finite-height theorem already exist as PAPER-A002. The 2016 Platt GRH paper concerns primitive Dirichlet characters with \(q\le400{,}000\) and separate explicit height formulas for odd and even moduli. Johansson's two works supply ball-arithmetic infrastructure and explicit Hurwitz-zeta remainder bounds. Hiary and Odlyzko–Schönhage supply evaluation methods and complexity bounds. [Platt 2017](https://doi.org/10.1090/mcom/3198) · [Platt–Trudgian 2021](https://doi.org/10.1112/blms.12460) · [Platt on GRH](https://doi.org/10.1090/mcom/3077) · [Johansson on Arb](https://doi.org/10.1109/TC.2017.2690633) · [Johansson on Hurwitz zeta](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11075-014-9893-1)

Chirre's line-one estimates illustrate what can transfer from a verified height. The proof controls the contribution from ordinates outside the verified range and restricts its conclusions to \(10^6\le t\le2.99997\cdot10^{12}\) when using \(T=3\cdot10^{12}\) and \(\delta=10^{-5}\). Brent, Platt and Trudgian's ordinate-sum estimate likewise includes an analytic tail identity for a specified weight class. In the separate Suzuki problem, bounded-time computations would need their own all-tail argument before yielding a global criterion. [Chirre](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0004972723001338) · [Brent–Platt–Trudgian](https://doi.org/10.1090/mcom/3652) · [Suzuki model review](../audits/adversarial_reviews/suzuki_model63_review.md)

## Full reading of Trudgian's second paper

The seven-page arXiv v2 was read in full. Theorem 1 gives
\[
\left|\int_{t_1}^{t_2} S(t)\,dt\right|
\le 1.698+0.183\log\log t_2+0.049\log t_2,
\qquad t_2>t_1>10^5 .
\]
Section 2 interpolates bounds across \([1/2,1]\) and \([1,1+\delta]\) using the entire function \((s-1)\zeta(s)\). Section 3 integrates those upper bounds, combines the result with the lower bound already in the 2011 paper, and gives the constants in Theorem 2. The \(k_2/4\) contribution in Lemma 2 comes from the exponent \(2k_2(1-\sigma)\) integrated over \([1/2,1]\). Theorem 1 uses the row labelled \(10^{10}\), with \(d=0.762\) and \(\delta=0.148\). The row label is the height near which that choice was tuned. The bound holds over the full stated range \(t_1>10^5\). The optimization table and cited zeta bounds received source reading. [arXiv v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.3416v2) · [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1216/RMJ-2016-46-1-325)

## Full reading of Saouter, Gourdon and Demichel

All seven printed pages 2281–2287 were read. The two central zero ordinates in Table 2 are approximately \(7954022502373.43289015387\) and \(7954022502373.43289494012\). The Lehmer-pair criterion uses the gap between \(H_0\) zeros, where \(x_j=2t_j\). Theorem B bounds the inverse-square interaction \(G_K\) using the adjacent zeros and a bound on the remaining zeros. In §5 an independent Riemann–Siegel/MPFR computation checks a 200-Gram-point neighborhood, and a Turing criterion with \(k=k'=35\) verifies the local count in the indicated interior range. This gives \(G_K\le379.1995\), \((x_{K+1}-x_K)^2G_K\le3.47471\cdot10^{-8}<4/5\), and the claimed strict lower bound \(\Lambda>-1.14541\cdot10^{-11}\). The paper reduces to RH and simple zeros for this argument because failure of RH gives \(\Lambda>0\), and a multiple zero at time zero gives \(\Lambda\ge0\) by the cited theory.

The authors describe Table 3 as an assessment under the GUE model. Its probabilities are evidence about how often gaps of this scale might occur. The local count and the quoted heat-flow criterion carry the lower-bound deduction. The computation, prior prefix input and cited inequalities were traced in the paper without independent numerical replay. [Saouter–Gourdon–Demichel](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-2011-02472-5) · [Csordas–Smith–Varga](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01205170)

## High samples and heat bounds

Odlyzko's report gives computed ordinates around index \(10^{22}\) with an explicit qualification about the rigor of absolute index localization. Its gap comparison in Figures 1–2 uses about a billion zeros beginning near index \(1.3\cdot10^{16}\), a different sample. Hiary–Odlyzko's derivative paper assumes RH in its formulation of the global statistics. Their moments paper reports substantial block-to-block variability even in high blocks containing roughly \(10^9\) zeros. Agreement with a random-matrix curve and visible deviations from that curve are both finite observations. The corpus already contains Odlyzko's 1987 spacings paper as PAPER-STAT62-003. [Odlyzko 2001](https://doi.org/10.1090/conm/290/04746) · [Derivative study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.4312v2) · [Moment study](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-2011-02573-1)

Polymath15 proves the upper bound \(\Lambda\le0.22\) in the \(e^{tu^2}\cos(zu)\) normalization. Theorem 1.2 combines initial zeta zero control through \(X/2\), an asymptotic zero-free region on the right at \(t_0\), and zero exclusion in a barrier between the left and right regions for \(0\le t\le t_0\). The choice \(t_0=y_0=0.2\) gives \(t_0+y_0^2/2=0.22\). In its §3.4, the already-recorded Platt–Trudgian paper uses the \(0.2\) row of the Polymath15 table together with its stronger finite-height theorem, yielding \(\Lambda\le0.2\) in Corollary 2. Combined with the existing Rodgers–Tao result \(\Lambda\ge0\) (PAPER-S004), those two cited papers give \(0\le\Lambda\le0.2\). Later certificate claims were outside this selection. [Polymath15, Theorems 1.1–1.2 and §10](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40687-019-0193-1) · [Platt–Trudgian, Corollary 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.09765) · [Rodgers–Tao](https://doi.org/10.1017/fmp.2020.6)
