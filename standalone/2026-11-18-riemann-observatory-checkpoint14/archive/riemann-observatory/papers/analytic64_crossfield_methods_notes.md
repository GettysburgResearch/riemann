## Whole-work readings

- **Guo, Li, Yung and Zorin-Kranich, arXiv:1912.09798v3, 11 pages.** Theorem 1.2 uses moment-curve boxes aligned to derivatives and an \(\ell^2\) sum. Corollary 1.3 yields the weighted Vinogradov mean value at \(p=k(k+1)\). Lemmas 2.2–2.3 reduce the linear estimate through a Whitney decomposition and affine rescaling. Lemmas 3.3–3.9 use uncertainty, transversality and a quotient by one tangent space to invoke decoupling in lower degree. Equation (4.5) eliminates the intermediate exponents to close the induction. Appendix A explicitly corrects weight exponents relative to the published version. Use the \(v3\) parameters in any attempt to reproduce the argument. [Pinned full source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.09798v3)
- **Guth, Solomon and Wang, arXiv:1904.05468v1, 17 pages.** The introduction gives counterexamples to a rich-ball estimate with only distinctness or angular spacing. Proposition 2.1 uses high-frequency Plancherel and low-frequency smoothing to separate an incidence bound from concentration in larger balls. Theorem 1.2 rescales a thin configuration and thickens a concentrated one. Theorem 4.1 uses separate small- and large-multiplicity inductions. In §5 the map from two separated planar points to a line of rigid motions transfers a position-spacing bound into a tube-spacing bound. That map is specific to the distance-quadruple problem. The printed statements of Theorems 1.1 and 1.3 display negative powers of \(W\) in the tube counts. The introduction, Theorem 4.1 and §5 use positive powers \(W^{2(n-1)}\). Proposition 2.1 also invokes disjointness within an angular class in its high-frequency proof, so any reuse must establish the needed within-class overlap control explicitly. [Pinned full source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.05468v1)
- **Matomäki and Teräväinen, arXiv:2403.13157v1, 16 pages.** Theorem 1.2 controls Lebesgue measure of a union over lengths. Section 5 shifts a Perron contour, splits off the small-height pole contribution and uses dyadic levels of \(|\zeta|\). Section 6 bounds \(\log\zeta\) in a zero-free rectangle using the log derivative and a smoothed prime sum. Section 7 uses the approximate functional equation to return to large polynomial values farther to the right. Section 8 uses the latter route for large value exponents and the density bound for small value exponents. This division is essential because the moving exponents can decay geometrically. Section 9 sketches the conditional short-interval application and §10 proves the forward direction with a mollifier, a discrete mean value estimate, and a functional-equation step that shortens long sums. For \(1-\nu<1/2+\varepsilon/2\), the stated invocation of Lemma 2.6 falls below its \(\sigma\) range. Theorem 1.2 in this boundary range follows directly from the trivial measure bound because \(\alpha=1/2\) is allowed and \(T^{\varepsilon+(1/2-(1-\nu))/2}N(1/2,CT)\gg T\), by symmetry and total zero counting. A separated-set application still needs its own comparison with Lebesgue measure. [Pinned full source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.13157v1)

## Transfer tests

1. A candidate curved embedding begins with \(\log(M+j)-\log M=\sum_{r=1}^{k}(-1)^{r-1}(j/M)^r/r+O_k((L/M)^{k+1})\) for \(0\le j\le L\le M/2\). For \(|t|\le T\), the phase error is \(O_k(T(L/M)^{k+1})\). This estimates the error when replacing the local phase by a polynomial, but the coefficient vector \((t/M,\ldots,(-1)^{k-1}t/(kM^k))\) still follows one line through the moment-curve dual domain. An application needs an inequality for that specific trace, including any thickening cost. For an incidence estimate, the corresponding test is a bound on the occupancy of every position-and-direction cell and quantitative control of how many original configurations map to the same incidence.
2. For the converse density result, measure of the large-value region and count of separated samples are different inputs. For a fixed dyadic block \((M,M']\), after demodulating by \(M^{it}\), the derivative is bounded by \(\sum_{M<n\le M'} n^{-1}\log(n/M)\ll 1\). Consequently a value of size \(M^{-\nu}\) persists at half its threshold on a neighborhood of radius \(\gg M^{-\nu}\). Using only this estimate to convert the measure bound to a count can lose \(M^\nu\). A sharper local persistence argument, or a directly discrete bound, is needed wherever that loss consumes the gain.
3. For short-interval Gowers inputs, keep the character approximants and exceptional sets through the arithmetic decomposition. A block selection depending on a large value or a zero needs a separate bound for how frequently that selection lies in the exceptional set. A polynomial approximation of \(t\log n\) also needs enough phase accuracy on the applicable \(H\) range.
4. The derivative-weighted prime-bias CLT uses fixed moments of a scalar statistic. The CUE and Gaussian-chaos results identify the extra estimates needed for a field or large-deviation claim. These include a two-point bound uniform through collisions, tightness under the actual sampling measure, and transform estimates in the tilt range.
